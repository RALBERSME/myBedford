Chapter 9:


Read the text aloud:



The icy stone walls of Bedford Gaol loomed menacingly against the grey sky. Clark was freezing. The wind bit into his face as he reached the heavy iron gate. It was a sinister place.

The guard behind the wicket had a face that looked as though it were carved from granite. Clark gave his name and demanded entry to speak with the prisoner, Edgar Tyler.
"Your papers, sir," the man grunted, without cracking a smile.

Clark reached into his coat pocket. But there was nothing. He cursed inwardly. Damn it all. In his haste, he had left his old badge with McAllroy, and he hadn't yet had him draw up a written warrant. Without proof, he was a nobody here. Just another onlooker at the prison gates.

"No ID, no entry," the guard said. The wicket slammed shut with a metallic clang.

Turned away, just like that. Clark stared at the cold iron in disbelief. He felt panic tightening around his throat; every second spent without Edgar slipped away unused, while the gallows waited. He whirled around to head back down the steps, empty-handed.

And there she stood. Liz!

Her cheeks were flushed from walking, her hair disheveled beneath her simple shawl. She stopped abruptly when she caught sight of Clark, recognition flashing instantly in her eyes. She grasped the situation with a single glance at his disappointed face. She acted immediately.

Liz stepped past him, straight up to the gate, and pounded her fist against the wood until the small wicket was yanked open again. "I am Liz Tyler!" she shouted at the sullen guard. Her voice was steady, though her fingers trembled. "Edgar Tyler is my brother. I have the right to see him. And this man here," she gestured sharply toward Clark, "is Mr. Rudney. Our family has retained him. He is conducting vital investigations for my brother’s defense and for the barrister, Mr. McAllroy. If you turn him away, you are obstructing the King's justice!"

The guard grunted something unintelligible, studying Liz’s determined face before casting a suspicious glance at Clark. The surname Tyler carried weight. A heavy ring of keys rattled on the other side. With a deep, grinding groan, the heavy gate opened a crack.

"Thirty minutes only," the warden growled, stepping aside. "Both of you, step inside."

Clark exhaled the breath he hadn't realized he was holding. He looked at Liz. A wave of relief washed the bitter cold from his limbs. His knees felt weak, and he felt a gratitude for this brave young lady so deep and overwhelming that for a moment, words failed him. Without her, he would have failed before his work had even begun.

"Thank you," he whispered. His gaze locked with hers for a long heartbeat before they stepped together into the grim corridor of the prison, which smelled of cold stone and disinfectant.

Once more, keys jingled. The sound cut sharply through the hallway. Then the guard threw the door open.

The visiting room turned out to be a bare, windowless box. There was nothing but grey plaster, reflecting the pale light of a dim gas lamp on the ceiling. In the center stood a wooden table, flanked by four chairs whose legs squeaked across the linoleum floor as they were moved.

Clark sat down. Liz followed suit. No sooner had they taken their seats than the door clicked shut into the lock once more.

Edgar walked in. His wrists were chained together; the metal of the handcuffs chafed coldly against his skin with every movement. The guard shoved him roughly forward until Edgar slumped onto the seat.

Liz bolted upright. With a yearning look, she stretched her arms out to Edgar.
"No touching!" the guard barked from the wall.
Liz froze. Slowly, she sank back down.

Clark looked at the boy. It was with a paralyzing sense of disbelief that he saw how Edgar was being treated here. But where was the evidence? There was none. Yet they handled him like a wild animal, like a convicted murderer for whom the noose was already being tied. It was utterly abhorrent.

Liz broke the silence. Her voice trembled. "Edgar, my dear Edgar. How are you? How are they treating you here?"

Edgar did not answer right away. His skin had taken on the color of cheap chalk. He looked shrunken. Usually, back at the sawmill, he would hoist fresh, ton-heavy logs onto his shoulders as if they were matchsticks. Now, those mountains of muscle seemed frozen and powerless. His fingers clawed into the edge of the tabletop, his knuckles showing white. He sought a handhold on the smooth wood. His hazel eyes stared blankly into space. Clark doubted whether the boy even registered the two of them. The rims of his eyelids were bloodshot and swollen.

He had been crying; there was no hiding it.

As if he had heard Clark’s silent thought, a heavy tear detached itself from the corner of Edgar's eye at that very moment, tracing a glistening path through the dirt on his cheek.

"I want to get out of here," he whispered. "I haven't hurt anyone. No one." His lower lip trembled uncontrollably. "I don't want to be hanged."

"We're here now," Liz murmured. She slid her hand slowly across the table. Inch by inch.

A harsh, threatening throat-clearing came from the door. The guard hadn't moved, but his glare practically impaled them. Liz flinched and pulled her fingers back as if she had just burned herself.

"This is Clark Rudney," she said hastily to fill the silence, gesturing to the man beside her. "He is an experienced investigator. He is going to get you out of here."

Clark leaned forward. He pulled a pencil and a crumpled notepad from his jacket pocket.

"Good day, Mr. Tyler. I am going to help you as best I can. I know you are innocent. I can see it in your eyes—your eyes cannot lie." He sought eye contact, fixing his gaze upon the boy’s pupils. "Mr. Tyler, what happened on the day in question? Please start from the morning."

Edgar’s larynx moved painfully. He swallowed dryly. "In the morning, I went to work as usual. Everything was normal. We were processing the logs from the last delivery. All of them into pit-props for the mining companies." He stared down at his hands. "Shortly before twelve, an urgent message arrived for George—I call my boss by his first name, you see. He ran into the office. When he came back out, he told me I should take the afternoon off. So I left during the lunch break. That was around one o'clock."

"And you don't know who the message was from?" Clark asked. His pencil hovered over the paper.

"No idea." Edgar shrugged. The metal of the chains rattled softly.

"Was your boss expecting someone?" Clark pressed further.

"I don't know that either. We weren't under any time pressure. There was no fixed delivery date for the rest of the week. You see, our clients always order well in advance. That gives us more leeway, so we don't have to rush the orders out at the last minute." He faltered. His breath came shallowly.

"Good. And after that, you went out into the meadows and fields, as Liz told me."

Edgar nodded mutely.

"Did anyone see you there? Or did you do anything that can be verified? For instance, dropping off a piece of mail somewhere, or something of the sort?"

The boy did not even have to think. "No, nothing. It wasn't until evening that my mother saw me come home. She was sitting on the steps outside my flat. But they say George was already dead by then..." His voice trailed off. His gaze darted fearfully toward the guard by the wall, as if he dreaded having said too much.

"Did you have trouble with your boss? Were there any arguments?" Clark wanted to know.

"Nothing. We hardly ever argued. George showed me what to do once, and ever since, he's just let me get on with it. I know the trade, after all. I learned it all during my apprenticeship, and I’ve been working at the sawmill for a good number of years now." A note of confusion crept into Edgar's voice. He did not understand what these questions were aiming at.

"Is Mr. Davies always at the mill when you're working?" Clark followed up.

"Yes, usually. The work can hardly be managed alone, after all."

"But this Mr. Davies is also a forester by trade, is he not? How can he spend so much time at the sawmill then?" Clark frowned. It made no sense.

"Well, we don't work every day. In the spring and autumn, we're only there two days a week. Otherwise, four. That's why I don't earn as much as the lads at Bedford’s big steam sawmill," Edgar clarified.

"That's where you did your apprenticeship, isn't it?" Clark confirmed, making a brief note. "And why did you leave?"

Edgar’s gaze drifted. He focused on a spot on the table. "They had a different work ethic there, that was all."

"Mr. Tyler, I am here because I want to convince the judge of..." Clark broke off mid-sentence. He bit his lip. The guard was standing too close. Any word about the judge was dangerous. If this warden was playing a double game and later reported every detail of the conversation, his strategy would be ruined.

He had to trust his intuition; it had rarely failed him. This terrified young man before him was no murderer. Despite the broad shoulders and the stature of a lumberjack, a frightened child sat there.

"Are you facing any trouble, Mr. Tyler? Debts? Women? Enemies?" Clark looked at him intently.

Liz shook her head vehemently. "My brother is an honest and conscientious man. I told you that already! He couldn't bring himself to hurt anyone!"

Clark raised his index finger to his lips. A sharp look cut her short. He leaned further across the table, close to Edgar’s face. "So, is there anything we need to know?"

"No. Nothing," Edgar defended himself. His voice grew louder, almost defiant. "But I’ve already told Mr. McAllroy, Aaron Baker, and that court psychologist all of this."

Clark paused. His pencil froze. "Court psychologist? A medical examiner has been here?"

He wanted to press for details, but the guard was already stepping forward. Heavy boots thudded against the floor. "Time's up."

The man grabbed Edgar by the upper arm and hauled him up. The handcuffs clanked as the boy was shoved back through the door into the cell block. The lock snapped shut with a heavy, final sound of locking.

Outside, the cold of the corridor greeted them once more. Liz and Clark stepped out of the heavy main portal of the prison. The wind blew mercilessly.

"What do we do now?" Liz asked. She was freezing, drawing her arms tight against her body. Clark did not answer. His thoughts were spinning in circles. A court psychologist? Here in Bedford? Small local gaols like this one didn't have permanent medical examiners for the state of mind. Where had the man come from? Who had sent him?

"Mr. Rudney?" Liz stepped closer and touched his sleeve.

"Um, yes. Right." He wrenched himself from his thoughts and looked down. With the toe of his shoe, he bored a deep hole into the damp gravel of the forecourt. "I think we should take a closer look at the crime scene."

They turned to leave.

Behind them, the hinges creaked. The heavy portal opened once more, and another guard stepped onto the stone steps. He looked around searchingly until he spotted the two of them. "Are you that investigator, the legal assistant?"

Clark whipped round. His nerves were frayed. "Yes, what is it?"

"An express courier from the public defender has just pulled up. Mr. McAllroy sends this urgent dispatch for you. The lad on the bicycle said it brooks no delay." The guard's tone was polite, almost businesslike, as he handed Clark the sealed letter.

Clark’s stomach tightened. A bad omen. He broke the seal, scanned the lawyer’s hasty lines, and Liz saw every ounce of life drain from his face.

It was not long before Clark folded the paper in disbelief, groping blindly for support. His fingers clawed into the rough, cold stone of the prison wall.

Every trace of color had vanished from his features. He looked exactly like Edgar inside the cell now—like a grey wall. He gasped for air, as though he had been dealt a blow to the pit of his stomach.

"They have performed the autopsy on the body," Clark brought out in choking gasps. His chest heaved heavily. A agonizing silence stretched between them.

Liz stared at him, her pupils dilated. "What is it? What did they find, Mr. Rudney?"

Clark pressed the flat of his hand against his forehead. He closed his eyes briefly, then reached for Liz’s hand. Her fingers were icy. "Liz, you must be strong now." He squeezed her hand tightly, almost painfully. "Fabric fibers have been identified both on the saw blade and in the victim’s wounds. They all match Edgar’s work jacket. The jacket that was lying on the log next to the saw."

A piercing scream cut through the dark alleyway. Liz’s eyes flew open. "That can’t be! That can’t be true!"

Clark reacted instantly. He grabbed her by the shoulders, pulled her along, and hurriedly shoved her around the next street corner. Just get out of here. Her screaming must not draw the prison guards back outside.

Only when they were sheltered by the wall of a building did he stop. His voice was nothing more than a harsh whisper. "It is, Liz. That is what forensics discovered. Edgar is well and truly in a bind now."

Gravel crunched beneath their soles as Clark walked rapidly onward. Liz could barely keep pace.

The road stretched out of the town. Here too, there seemed to be nothing today but desolate fields beneath a heavy, grey sky. Clark used the march to brief Liz; the contents of McAllroy’s dispatch weighed on him like lead.

"It was definitely no accident," Clark said, cold sweat running down his collar. "There was a struggle. Davies was already dead before he even touched the saw blade." He did not look at her, his gaze scanning the distant horizon. "They found severe bruising all over his upper body. It likely came from a blunt metal object—an iron bar or something similar. Someone beat him savagely."

Brutal, raw violence.

"That rules out Pat O’Neill," he stated dryly.

Liz stumbled but caught herself at once. "What? Pat? Was she under suspicion too?" Disbelief was written across her face. Pat, the petite postal worker who always cycled so cheerfully through the streets in the morning? The thought was utterly absurd.

"They suspect everyone," Clark’s voice replied, cuttingly. "Anyone who can’t produce an alibi in time."

They finally reached the sawmill. Stacks of timber rose before them like barricades, and the scent of damp sawdust hung heavy and musty in the air. They crept flat against the side of the building.

"Do you know your way around here, Liz? Have you been here before?"

"Of course." She nodded eagerly. "My brother works here, have you forgotten? I often brought him his lunch."

"Good. Then tell me..." Clark swallowed the word as they turned the corner of the building.

The yard was cordoned off with heavy ropes that swung in the cold wind. They were stretched taut from the entrance of the house across the grounds. Two police constables stood there, hands on their utility belts, as watchful as lynxes.

Clark did not hesitate. Casting his nervousness aside, he walked straight up to the first officer. "I thought the gentlemen from the Criminal Investigation Department were already finished with their work," he said conversationally.

The constable eyed him coldly. "They are."

"Then why the guard?" Clark pressed, gesturing to the cordon.

"Orders from Sergeant Baker. You never know what's been overlooked. Better safe than sorry," the man replied with a shrug. "Pure routine."

"Better safe than sorry," Clark muttered. He reached into his coat pocket, but his credentials as legal assistant for the defense were still lying on McAllroy’s desk. A novice mistake that he was paying for now for the second time. He tried anyway; it was his last straw. "I am the official legal counsel for Edgar Tyler’s defense. May I have a quick look around?"

"No." The constable shook his head, and Clark realized at once that there was no room for negotiation. "That goes against orders."

It was futile; Clark recognized the wall he was running into. He forced a noncommittal smile, nodded to the man, and offered Liz his arm. They turned away from the sentries and walked briskly off.

"Don’t look back," Clark whispered in her ear. They only slowed their pace once the corner of the building shielded them from the guards’ gaze.

There Clark stopped. His breath came in spurts. "Isn’t it all bloody strange?" He didn’t even wait for her reply. "It’s more than strange."

He turned back to the building. "Liz, what exactly is this wing? Did Davies live here?"

"No," she whispered back. "There is only the office here. A small kitchen, a large room for the bookkeeping where there's also a sofa. Sometimes Davies stayed the night here, but Edgar said that hardly ever happened. He actually lives entirely at the forester’s lodge."

Clark stepped up to the windowpane. He placed his palms against his temples and pressed his nose against the cold glass. Inside, there was pure chaos. Files lay on the floor, papers had been torn out and scattered across the wooden floorboards like withered leaves. In the corner stood a desk, its drawers all wide open.

"Someone was searching in there," Clark muttered, a shiver running down his neck. "For something bloody important, it seems to me. Liz, do you have any idea what it could be?"

She shook her head helplessly. "Edgar and I... we never talked about old Davies. He was over sixty. He could easily have been our grandfather."

"Was he married? Did he have family?"

"No. As far as I know, he lived entirely alone at the forester’s lodge."

Clark stepped back from the window. His mind was made up. "If we can’t get in here, we’ll just have to get our answers there. Where is this lodge?"

"To the southeast," she said promptly. "In Chicksands Wood. That’s nearly ten kilometers from here." She fumbled with her bag. "Shall I draw the way for you?"

Clark faltered. "Aren’t you coming with me?"

Liz looked down, her cheeks flushing with shame. "I still have an order to finish at the weaving mill today. We need every shilling, Mr. Rudney. For Mr. McAllroy’s fee and the court costs." She looked up again. "Or shall we go together tomorrow morning?"

"No. That’s too late. We have no time," Clark shook his head. Every day counted. "I’ll manage on my own. The sooner we find evidence, the better."

Liz pulled a small stub of a pencil from her pocket and sketched the path on the back of an old receipt. With a few jagged lines, she indicated the edge of the woods and marked the forester’s lodge.

They walked back in silence as far as Midland Road. There, their paths diverged. Liz turned toward the smoking chimneys of the mills, while Clark turned up his collar. Before him lay the long, lonely march to Chicksands Wood. A ten-kilometer walk—and twilight was already threatening on the horizon.
