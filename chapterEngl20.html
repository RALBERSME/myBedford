Chapter 20:


Read the text aloud:



The ticking of the grandfather clock was like a hammer. Stroke after stroke, driving straight into her forehead. For four days, life had stood still. Grey dust danced in the pale light filtering through the lace curtains into Eyleen’s living room. Liz stared at her hands. Her fingertips were rough, her nails short. Outside, in the adjacent building, the door to her little sewing shop remained locked. A handwritten sign hung in the window: Closed due to illness. But it wasn’t the flu. It was the uncertainty. It gnawed through her nerves, paralyzed her fingers, made every straight needle stitch impossible.

“It’s the air in here,” a voice rang out from the chaise longue. “It’s suffocating me. It’s killing me, Liz. But of course, you don’t care.”

Eyleen Newland lay on the cushions in her silk dressing gown. She held a hand to her forehead, her fingers elegantly splayed—just as they had been in the London theatre, back when she still sang the leading roles. Her hair had been painstakingly teased, but it was slowly losing its hold.

“I brought you some tea, Mother,” Liz said quietly. Her voice sounded flat, drained.

“Tea!” Eyleen snorted without opening her eyes. “As if chamomile could heal a mother’s broken heart. A bleeding heart, Liz! My Edgar. My divine, innocent boy. They want to slaughter him. Like an animal. And I must lie here and watch my beauty, my entire talent, rot away in this swamp of anxiety.”

With a sudden jerk, she sat up. Her eyes, once the pride of the tabloid press, flashed angrily at Liz. “It’s easy for you to talk. You just sit there. In silence. You have no idea what it means to be an artist. A mother! Every fibre of my body screams it aloud: Edgar is innocent! He couldn't have killed anyone. It’s biologically impossible. He has my genes. The sensitivity. The elegance.”

“We just don’t know, Mother,” Liz whispered, pressing her knees together. “McAllroy says the court…”

“McAllroy is a hack!” Eyleen cut her off shrilly. She threw a hand into the air—a dramatic gesture straight from the opera stage. “A provincial lawyer with stains on his lapels. He understands nothing of our family’s tragedy. And you? You’re probably still defending him. Just like that other failure. That… that Clark Rudney!”

At the sound of the name, Liz flinched. A cold shiver ran down her spine.

“Clark tried to help, Mother. He’s a social worker, he…”

“He is a nobody!” Eyleen spat. She sprang to her feet, her silk gown rustling furiously. She began to pace up and down the room, her head held high as if fixing her gaze on the audience in the theatre boxes. “Good for nothing, that's what he is. A minor clerk wallowing in the filth of the underclass, imagining he knows something about life. And then the sheer insolence of it! That absolute, boundless disrespect.”

She paused, turned on her heel, and pointed a manicured finger at Liz. “He dared to court me. Me! The great Eyleen Newland! He looked at me with those hungry little eyes. Thought a lonely widow from the theatre would sink gratefully into his arms, no doubt. A man who reeks of cheap tobacco and government offices. Revolting. An absolute, crawling parasite.”

Liz lowered her head. Her chest tightened. She didn’t understand. Clark had always been there. His hands had touched her, his voice had given her security when everything was crashing down around them. He had listened to her. Truly listened. No one else had ever done that.

But why was he gone now? Why hadn't he reached out when Edgar was arrested? Had it all been a lie? Had he really just wanted to worm his way close to her mother, as Eyleen claimed? The doubts drilled into her mind like tiny, poisonous needles.

“I thought… I thought he was being sincere,” Liz murmured.

“Sincere?” Eyleen burst into a bright laugh. A theatrical, hollow laugh that echoed in the corners of the room. “You are so touchingly naive, Elisabeth. Unimaginative and naive. That is why you sit in that tiny sewing shop, altering the dresses of women who don’t have half my class. You don’t see the world for what it is. Men like that scent our weakness. He wanted to bask in my glory. When he realized there was nothing to be gained from me—that I saw right through him!—he bolted. He abandoned us. Edgar is in mortal danger, the gallows await, and that cowardly dog is hiding.”

Eyleen trat dicht an Liz heran. Sie roch nach schwerem Puder und abgestandenem Parfüm. Sie legte eine Hand auf Liz’ Schulter, doch es war kein Trost. Der Griff war fest, fordernd.

Eyleen stepped close to Liz. She smelled of heavy powder and stale perfume. She placed a hand on Liz’s shoulder, but there was no comfort in it. The grip was firm, demanding.“You need to finally wake up,” Eyleen hissed. “You have to be there for me. Who is going to catch me if my Edgar… if they take him from me? You have no one, after all. No career, no husband. You only have me. And yet you sit around here like a wet rag, mourning some social worker. You should be thanking me for chasing that man out of our lives.”

Liz felt the air leave her lungs. She wanted to argue. She wanted to say that Clark had saved her when the mob stood outside the house. But the words caught in her throat. Eyleen’s presence filled the room completely. There was no space left for Liz.

“I’m just scared for Edgar,” Liz said finally, as a single tear broke free and rolled down her cheek.

Eyleen let go of her, waved her hand dismissively, and turned back to the mirror. She stroked her cheekbones, checking her profile. “We are all scared, Elisabeth. But some of us must carry the weight of the entire world on our shoulders. Go into the kitchen. Bring me fresh water. This has long since grown warm.”

Liz stood up mechanically. Her legs felt like lead. She took the glass. As she stepped into the kitchen, her hands shook so violently that the glass struck the stone sink. A faint, snapping sound. A tiny crack in the glass. Just like the one inside her.

The glass didn’t shatter. It only cracked. A fine, hairline fracture snaked through the bottom, but it held. Liz stared at it. She wasn’t breathing. Her heart hammered against her ribs like a wild, caged bird. She filled the damaged glass with tap water, watched the air bubbles rise to the top, and carried it back into the living room. Every step she took felt like running a gauntlet.

Eyleen was back on the chaise longue. She was filing her nails now. The rhythmic, scraping sound set Liz’s teeth on edge.

“You took forever,” Eyleen said without looking up. “Daydreaming again, I suppose. As usual.”

Liz set the glass down on the small side table. Too forcefully. A few drops splashed onto the polished mahogany.

Eyleen let out a sharp shriek. She flung the nail file onto the carpet. “Have you lost your mind? My table! You’re ruining everything, Elisabeth. Everything I have left! First, your mere existence ruined my career, and now you’re destroying my last remaining possessions.”

That was the spark. Something snapped inside Liz. The four days of paralysis, the leaden dread for Edgar, the toxic talk about Clark—it all coiled into a burning lump in her throat. She didn’t pull her hand back. She stood her ground, straight as an arrow.

“I am not ruining anything,” Liz said. Her voice no longer trembled. It was cold. Quiet. “It’s just three drops of water, Mother.”

Eyleen froze and slowly raised her head. Her eyes narrowed into two thin, hateful slits. This was no longer theatre. This was naked, narcissistic rage. Someone dared to contradict her. The star. The martyr.

“I beg your pardon?” Eyleen breathed. “What did you just call me?”

“Mother,” Liz repeated, taking a step forward. “You talk about nothing but yourself. For days. Edgar is sitting over in his flat, hardly daring to breathe because he’s terrified of the gallows. And you? You’re wailing about your career. About your youth. About a social worker who supposedly courted you. Do you have any idea how ridiculous that is?”

Eyleen sprang up. Her cheeks were flush with splotches of red beneath the thick powder. Suddenly she seemed larger, more menacing—like an approaching storm front

“Ridiculous?” she shrieked. “You call me ridiculous? You tiny, insignificant little nothing! You’ve never stood in the limelight. You don’t know what it means to have the world at your feet. Clark begged me! He devoured me with his eyes while pretending to care about Edgar’s files. He wanted to use me to climb the social ladder. And you are so blind, so pathetically jealous, because no man will ever look at you that way!”

The blow hit Liz square in the face. She felt her knees go weak, but she forced herself to remain standing. The humiliation burned on her skin.

“He wanted to help us, Mother,” Liz whispered. The truth simply forced its way out.

Eyleen laughed again — a hideous, shrill sound. She stepped so close to Liz that her breath fell warm against Liz’s forehead.

“Help?” Eyleen scoffed. She eyed her daughter from head to toe, her gaze filled with utter contempt. “Just look at you, Elisabeth. You wear the hand-me-down dresses from my extra roles. You smell of tailor’s chalk and cheap soap. Why would a man like Clark want to help you? You are the shadow in this house. You’re only here to bring me tea and dry my tears. You are the reason for everything that went wrong in my life. Don’t flatter yourself. If Clark told you he wanted to help, it was out of pure pity. Because he felt sorry for you, like a filthy stray dog.”

The words hit home. They stabbed deep into Liz’s heart, right where that tiny, secret hope for Clark had lived. Had it been pity? Just pity? The doubts exploded in her head. She saw Eyleen’s triumphant face. Her mother was relishing her victory. She was feasting on the destruction of her daughter.

Liz took a deep breath. Her hand tightened into a fist.

You are a monster, Liz thought. Very quietly. No tears, just a hardened gaze.

Eyleen raised her hand as if to strike. Then she paused. Her fingers clawed at the air. “Out,” she hissed. “Get out of my sight. Go to your sewing shop and rot there among your scraps of fabric. You deserve this pain. You know nothing of life.”

Liz turned around. She didn’t run. She walked with slow, mechanical steps toward the door. She reached for her coat, ripped it from the hook, and rushed out into the grey street. The front door slammed shut behind her.

Outside, she was cold. The wind cut into her face. But the air was pure—free of Eyleen’s heavy perfume. Liz walked down the street, her hands buried deep in her pockets. Her mother’s words echoed in her mind, an endless, torturous loop. Out of pure pity.

She didn’t know where to go. Aimlessly, she let her feet carry her away.
