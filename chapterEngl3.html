Chapter 3:


Read the text aloud:



When he returned, his apartment smelled of cold coffee and dust. Clark stared out the window. Outside, figures moved through the dirt of the construction site, lifting shovels, calling out to one another. But Clark did not see them. In his mind, there was room only for Eyleen. He had to reach her — somewhere, somehow.

"A minstrel," the thought flashed abruptly in his mind. "A modern singer beneath her balcony, with a song on his lips that would bring tears to the eyes of a former actress." He could already picture himself right there beside her.

He sat down at the wooden table. The Remington was already waiting. His fingers began to pound away. The metal clattered in a staccato rhythm against the platen:


When the Sun Rises on Midland Road – by Clark Rudney

Yesterday, the world stood still. In the midst of the dusty asphalt, in the midst of the city haze, you were suddenly there. Your gait had none of the heaviness of these streets. You swept the gray away. I stood in the shadow of Hartley’s masonry ledge, the bricks cold against my back, and forgot to breathe. At that moment, my own life felt like an empty shell; my whole, gray life ceased to exist in that very second. I want to see you. To look into your eyes for just a fleeting moment. Let me be near you, just this once, for you have thrown my heart out of rhythm. All the beauty of this world is united in your loveliness.

The paper rustled as he yanked it from the machine with a swift jerk. His palms were damp. He skimmed the lines, read them once more, and pressed his lips to the paper. Then he ran off.

Midland Road reeked of soot and cheap grease, but Clark didn't care. He hurried forward, scanning the storefronts for "Hebblethwaite’s Toys." When he reached the toy shop, a smile of relief flickered across his lips. His eyes locked onto the row of doorbells beside the shop. There were six brass plates, but none bore the name Newland. He rubbed his chin; the stubble scratched beneath his fingers.

Behind him, a lock clicked. A young woman stepped out onto the pavement. She wore a beige sewing smock, its cut as unremarkable as the gray of the house wall. Her hair was light, her eyes dark. Keeping her gaze lowered, she was about to slip past him, but Clark stepped into her path.

"Do you live here?" Clark asked searchingly.

The woman startled. Her fingers gripped the fabric of her apron. "Yes. Why?"

"The actress, Eyleen Newland. Does she live in this building?"

"Ring for Tyler," she whispered. Her gaze darted nervously from his shoes to his face. A moment later, she squeezed past him and disappeared behind the door of the adjacent building.

Clark exhaled. He raised his index finger and pressed the plate engraved with "E. Tyler."

Nothing.

He pressed again. This time, he held his finger against the metal a little longer. Still nothing. He took two steps back onto the cobblestones, tilting his head back. The second-floor windows remained dark. But there — ever so faintly, the curtain upstairs had moved, barely more than a tremor in the fabric.

She was there. That was enough for him.

He reached into his pocket, pulled out the crumpled sheet, and took a deep breath. His voice echoed off the facades, sonorous, almost theatrical. He read. The words flew up toward the motionless curtain. Beads of sweat formed on his forehead, trickling down and stinging his eyes. His hands shook so violently that the paper crackled in his fingers. As the last word died away, nothing remained but the dull rumble of the city. The window stayed shut. No one showed themselves.

Minutes passed. Clark kept looking up, time and again. But nothing stirred anymore. After what felt like an endless wait, he gave up. With stiff fingers, he smoothed the paper, folded it once, twice, and slid it into the slot of the mailbox.

He pulled out his handkerchief to dry his face. His gaze wandered along the building. Only now did he notice something fluttering to the right, on the neighboring house. A small, wooden sign hung there: Liz Tyler – Needlework. The door to the small workshop stood open, offering a glimpse inside. It was quite dark within, a narrow room packed with bolts of fabric. "That is exactly where the young woman fled," Clark remembered. "Had she heard everything?"

A hollow feeling spread in his stomach, as if he had swallowed sour milk. He turned around and walked away. His legs quickly carried him off.

Hardly had his footsteps faded when the heavy front door next to the toy shop opened again. Eyleen stepped onto the pavement. Her chin was held high, her gaze fixed straight ahead. In her hand, she held the folded paper. She did not spare the street a single glance, but headed straight for the needlework shop and walked in.

"Hello, Liz."

Liz put down her scissors. She jumped up from her stool so hastily that it nearly toppled over. In the back of the shop, with a hurried, erratic movement, she opened the wardrobe, revealing a heavy, dark fabric.

"Your new dress. It's finished." Liz lifted the piece as if it were made of delicate porcelain. "Deep navy blue. Exactly the shade of the Union Jack."

Eyleen grabbed the dress. Her fingers slid over the silk probingges, almost suspiciously. "About time. The performance is tomorrow," she snorted drily and vanished with the dress behind the changing booth curtain.

Liz used the time to push a heavy wooden pedestal in front of the three-way mirror. Her knees trembled slightly. Holding her breath, she waited for the rustle of the fabric from the booth. She hoped for a word, perhaps a compliment.

Eyleen stepped out and climbed onto the pedestal. She spun on her axis. Once. Twice. The silk flowed loosely through the room, and a piece of the hem became visible. Immediately, Liz knelt before her mother. The pincushion was tight around her wrist, three pins already held between her lips. She made herself small, staring only at the hem, trying to take up as little space as possible in her own shop.

"It shortens me," the voice said from above in a cold tone. Eyleen studied herself in the mirror, hands pressed flat against her hips. "The waist is wrong. I look wide. Do you want me to look like a pufferfish?"

Liz took a pin out of her mouth. Her fingertips were dotted with tiny red marks from old pricks. She reached for the fabric at the hip. "The fabric sits on the hip line, Mother. If I take it in any tighter, it will bunch up when you walk."

"Then you cut it wrong," Eyleen hissed without looking at her. Her chin drifted even higher. "Incredible. You work with fabrics every single day. But a feel for a woman's silhouette? No, you simply lack the eye for it."

The sentence hit Liz like a blow to the stomach. It was not new. It was so painfully familiar: "You lack the eye for it."

A dull ache spread through her chest. This accusation reminded her of who she was. She did not belong to the world of the stage, not in the spotlight. She was only the one who cleaned up other people's messes. The one who sewed the dresses in which her mother shone.

Liz stared at the hem of the blue dress. She breathed heavily, then carefully stuck pins into the fabric. Her fingers were shaking. A tiny red dot formed on the blue silk. Blood, she realized with a jolt. She quickly wiped it onto her own smock before Eyleen could notice.

"I will open up the hip seam," Liz whispered toward the floor. "It will be corrected by tomorrow morning."

"I should hope so," Eyleen said imperiously. She stepped off the pedestal without gracing Liz with a glance. The dress rustled violently as she pulled it over her head. Back in her everyday clothes, she carelessly tossed the blue silk onto the cutting table and pulled her powder compact from her handbag.

At that exact moment, the folded paper slipped out of Eyleen’s bag. It drifted to the floor, landing directly at Liz’s feet. Eyleen didn't notice. She snatched up her bag, cast one last critical glance into the mirror, and walked toward the door. "I expect you at my apartment tomorrow morning at seven with the dress. On time."

The shop door bell chimed. Eyleen was gone.

Liz remained on her knees. The silence in the shop was hard to bear. Her gaze wandered to the paper on the floorboards. Her hand vibrated as she reached for the sheet. With a single movement, she unfolded it. The typewriter ink was deep black, slightly smudged in a few places by the author’s sweat:

"… I stood in the shadow of Hartley’s masonry ledge … my whole, gray life ceased to exist in that very second."

It was the poem. The poem the young man had recited in the street just a few minutes ago, his voice trembling. He had stared up at Eyleen’s window while Liz had stood behind the bolts of fabric, soaking in every single word.

Liz read the lines again and again. A sorrowful smile stole onto her lips. He had written these words for the queen of the stage. For the woman who had just branded Liz’s work as "fabulously incompetent." He was searching for diamonds in the eyes of a woman who possessed nothing but coldness.

She turned the sheet over. On the back, a grease stain with the imprint of lips was visible. Liz couldn't help but smile faintly. Slowly, she stood up. Her limbs were stiff and aching. She placed the poem on the table next to the unfinished blue silk. Tomorrow, she would bring the dress to her mother. And perhaps, she thought as she picked up the scissors, she would write a letter back to the newly minted author. A letter from reality.

Liz took the seam ripper. The blade flashed in the pale light. Seam by seam, she released the thread from the blue silk. She felt every faint tear in her fingertips.

The hours passed. Outside, it grew dark. Midland Road slept. Only in the needlework shop did a solitary light still burn.

Liz pinned the fabric anew. She draped it, faster than before. Her fingers were no longer bleeding. The ache in her stomach had vanished, replaced by a cold, clear fatigue. Time and again, her eyes fell upon the poem lying next to the sewing machine.

"All the beauty of this world is united in your loveliness."

Liz let out a soft laugh—a rough, dry sound. Clark knew nothing. What did he possibly know about musical theater actresses? Was he that blinded by her beauty? Surely, he did not yet know the venom behind the scenes.

She slid the blue silk under the presser foot. The rhythmic rattling of the machine filled the room. Faster and faster, the needle raced across the hem until, finally, the final stitch was cast. The silhouette was now flawless. The waist narrow, elegant. A dress for a queen. Sewn by a slave.

It was three o'clock in the morning when she stopped the machine. Liz snipped the last thread. She rose, smoothed the blue silk fabric, and hung the dress on the dress form. It looked beautiful. Almost painfully beautiful.

She stepped to the cutting table, took a sheet of cheap, gray wrapping paper, and grabbed a pencil. Her hand did not hesitate. She wrote:


To the minstrel of Midland Road.

The queen does not listen. She only sees the mirror. Their eyes are not diamonds, but glass. Cold, cut glass. If you seek beauty, Mr. Rudney, do not look on the second floor. Only yesterday's applause lives there.

A gray mouse.


But then she changed her mind. She tore up the note and quickly burned it.

Instead, she took the paper she had picked up from the floorboards. She folded it tightly and slid it into a fold of the deep blue dress—right on the inside of the waist, exactly where the pins had been before.

Tomorrow, Eyleen would wear the dress. Tomorrow, the paper would rustle.

Liz turned out the light.
