Chapter 15:


Read the text aloud:



The minute hand of the pocket watch jumped to five o'clock. A metallic click, then silence returned to Clark’s palm. In the sky above Bedford, thick, sulfur-yellow clouds gathered, pressing the chimney soot back down into the streets. The cold June wind drove sticky street grime before it. Clark felt the leather of his bicycle grips—damp and slippery under his clammy fingers. He needed those case files. Every fiber of his being craved the transcripts, the notes of that sinister court psychiatrist. He had to know what had happened to Edgar Tyler behind the closed doors of Horne Lane.

His rejection by the police had been short and sharp.
"Caseworker? Sorry, Edgar Tyler’s of age, he don’t need no caseworker," the constable at the counter had barked. His voice was as dry as sandpaper. A condescending glance at Clark’s scuffed shoes, and then the heavy wooden door slammed shut. The wind howled through the alleyway. Clark stood on the pavement, his teeth clenched tight.

He considered his options. Should he ask Eyleen for help? But he dismissed the thought immediately. The police would raise the exact same wall, whether he showed up with her or not. They would simply cite Edgar’s legal age again, and that would be the end of it. Edgar’s sentence was essentially a foregone conclusion before the trial had even begun. A mother’s word counted for nothing in Bedford against a transcript of the Crown. Clark’s thumb rubbed nervously over the bicycle's brake lever. His knees trembled slightly at the growing certainty that something was rotten here. A systematic diversion tactic—he could feel it in his bones. The officers were stonewalling to keep the dirt swept under the carpet. And the public, of course, must never find out.

The trial was tomorrow.

The files were likely long gone from the station. Clark suspected they were over at Shire Hall on St. Paul’s Square, where the Assize Courts held sway. For ten minutes, he pedaled hard once more, passing the gray faces of factory workers on their way home. Then the monument loomed before him: a massive nightmare of dark red brick and grotesque terracotta ornaments in the Gothic Revival style. The walls were blackened by industrial coal dust that had eaten into every crevice over decades. Pointed gables thrust into the overcast sky like stone lances. This was Shire Hall. On the ground floor, behind the deep window lintels, sat the bureaucracy of the County Police.

But how on earth was he supposed to get inside this fortress?

Clark brushed his wet hair from his forehead. The underground coal chute Richard had told him about was too risky. That left the windows facing the side alley. His eyes scanned the facade, stone by stone. Suddenly, he caught his breath. He noticed a narrow gap: one of the ground-floor sash windows was open about a hand's breadth. A thick, bluish cloud billowed out in bursts, losing itself in the river mist. The sweet, heavy stench of cheap Virginia tobacco hit him. The constables inside were clouding themselves in properly.

His heart hammered against his ribs; he felt a dull ache. Clark had never broken the law in his life, let alone trespassed into a government building. His upbringing rebelled against every step. But Richard wouldn't have hesitated. Richard was no dawdler. He would have pushed the window open and leaped.

Clark swallowed hard. Crossing himself with a quick, agitated movement, he pulled himself up onto the building's ledge and swung his legs over the window frame.

The din of the room enveloped him instantly; a deafening staccato struck his ears. Half a dozen typists were hammering away at heavy Imperial typewriters at a furious pace. The metallic clack of the typebars and the shrill ding at the end of each line completely swallowed the sound of his landing. Not one of the women looked up. Their fingers flew across the keys, their faces fixed staring at their shorthand notebooks. The corridor was only a few feet away, right behind the desks.

Clark’s hand went instinctively into his trousers pocket. His fingers brushed against a small object wrapped in shiny tin foil. A smile crept onto his face. Drawing his shoulders back, he put on a mask of absolute confidence and stepped forward. Passing the desk of the nearest typist, he raised his eyebrows and dropped a casual, British "Good evening, Miss." At that exact moment, the strictly rationed peppermint sweet slipped from his hand and landed with a soft click beside her typewriter. The woman blinked in surprise, but by then Clark had already turned the handle of the corridor door and slipped into the hallway. The wood clicked shut behind him.

He had cleared the first hurdle.

The long corridor in which he now stood smelled of the harsh austerity of floor polish. Clark knew how the machinery worked here. Those men in uniform didn't care about a culprit's psyche. They needed a confession—a signed piece of paper for the files, no matter the pressure under which it had been extracted. A murder interrogation was not a psychological consultation; it was mental torture. Hours of questioning in windowless cellars, sleep deprivation, no lawyers, until the suspect's will broke. The transcripts were hastily taken down in shorthand and later supplemented from memory. But with the court psychiatrist in the picture, there was bound to be more to it. There was a second truth.

As he walked down the passage, he scanned the door signs.
"Miller. Cooper. Turner," he read. The corridor seemed endless. "Green. Carter." Where was that bloody name? "Fletcher. Goddard." At the very end, almost in the shadow of the stairwell, he spotted the desired plaque: "Sergeant A. Baker."

Clark cast a panicked look over his shoulder. But the hallway was still empty. Nothing could be heard but the distant echo of the typewriters. Carefully, he tried the handle of Baker’s office. It wouldn't budge. Locked, Clark realized. Damn it.

His gaze fell upon a huge, sprawling parlor palm in the corner of the corridor. Its fan-like, dark green leaves formed a perfect, opaque screen. Behind it, a narrow hallway window was just barely visible. Clark squeezed behind the rough stems of the plant, pushed the window sash upward, and poked his head out into the open air. Risking a look to his left, he found what he was looking for: Baker’s sash window was also open a crack. "The sergeant clearly likes to smoke his pipe by an open window," he muttered. "But that's just as well." He looked down at the narrow window ledge. A single misstep meant a fall into the paved courtyard below — though from the first floor, that would be survivable in a pinch.

He squeezed himself through the frame, pressed his back against the damp, cold brickwork, and moved inch by inch along the wall of the building. With the toe of his boot, he nudged Baker’s window further up, gripped the frame, and slid silently into the office.

The room breathed discipline. It looked like Prussian order in an English guise. Not a single loose sheet of paper lay around. A black Bakelite telephone sat enthroned on the hefty oak desk. Beside it stood a wooden pen holder, an untouched notepad, and a heavy briar pipe resting on a green marble ashtray. A solitary silver frame caught his attention. Clark stepped closer and picked up the photograph. A heavily pregnant woman looked back at him. She had thick, black curly hair and dark, deep eyes that exuded something strangely sensual. A true beauty, captured in a moment of waiting, Clark thought, replacing the frame exactly where it had been. He had no intention of leaving any traces behind.

The wardrobe contained nothing but the service uniform and a light-colored duffle coat. For his purpose, it was all useless gear. Clark turned to the low sideboard next to the armchair. The drawer slid open with a faint squeak. It housed a suspension file system, sorted alphabetically. His fingers flew through the cardboard tabs. There — a thick, beige folder bore Edgar Tyler’s name. It was written in bold black ink across the cardboard.

Clark flipped the cover open. Loose pages and typewritten reports about the boy's upbringing and background slid out toward him. Yet, an official interrogation transcript from the psychiatrist was nowhere to be found. He leafed through the pages in a hurry, his fingers trembling noticeably now. Suddenly, a small, inconspicuous scrap of paper slipped from between the sheets of the file. It was scribbled with dark ink in a hasty, almost illegible hand. Clark narrowed his eyes to decipher the hieroglyphs:
"Isolation in darkness. Sodium Amytal every 4 hours."

His breath caught in his throat. Sodium Amytal. What on earth was that? Edgar was physically healthy; what purpose could this medical treatment possibly serve?

Heavy footsteps now echoed from the corridor. It was the unmistakable clicking of heels on the floor tiles. Then voices approached. One of them was loud, booming, and thick with arrogant authority. Aaron Baker. Clark recognized that voice instantly—it was the very same voice that had covered up the bus accident on the day of his arrival in Bedford.

With flying fingers, Clark shoved the note under his shirt, directly against his bare skin. The folder flew back into the filing drawer. Then he slammed the drawer shut. A soft click rang out. Swiftly, Clark threw himself out of the window, legs first, just as the sergeant’s bunch of keys clattered metallically against the lock of the office door down the hall.
