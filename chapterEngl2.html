Chapter 2:


The rattling of jackhammers roared deafeningly through the closed window. Clark jolted awake. The clock showed just past seven.

Across the street, an excavator was dismantling an old brick house. Debris fell by the second, kicking up a thick dust that settled like grey powder on the windowsill. Clark dragged himself to the pane, his eyelids still heavy with sleep. Had those monsters been standing there yesterday? He couldn’t remember them. A heavy tipper truck roared to life, the driver grinding through the gears. The windowpane in Clark’s room vibrated.

Clark’s face paled. So here, here is where I'm supposed to write, it occurred to him. He stared down at grey Bedford, his teeth clenched tight. His shoulders slumped for a moment, but then he caught himself. "No. If those men down there are slogging away in the dirt, I’m not sitting around here idling," he decided. "Above all, I need caffeine. Right now."

He returned with a mug full of steaming black brew. The bitter aroma rose into his nostrils. On the sideboard, he had placed a small frame. It was a farewell photograph from Luton. His fingers trembled slightly as he touched it.

The picture showed him in the middle of a cluster of teenagers with wild hair and dirty knees. Memories surged within him. He could practically see them before him—his boys. Kicking a football on the asphalt, getting into fights, and hiding pocket knives in their trousers. Stealing tobacco at the market, breaking into cellars. But despite all that, Clark had seen something in every single one of them: future, dedicated fathers who would support their families through honest work.

Clark had fought for them, relentlessly.

For those boys, he had stood for hours in cold corridors outside courtrooms. He had argued with stubborn officials, talking as if his life depended on it, just to keep them out of Borstal youth prison. When his knees threatened to buckle completely, he would seek backing from older colleagues. He had built a youth club, putting table tennis tables into drafty cellars just to get the lads off the streets. He had secured apprenticeships at Vauxhall Motors, slipping into the factory halls to keep an eye on the foremen so no one would exploit the boys. Twenty-four hours a day, that had been his life. There had been no time for women. In truth, there had been no life of his own at all, yet everything he did had felt so meaningful.

Until the world shattered.

A lump formed in Clark’s throat. Suddenly, his eyes burned painfully. A single tear broke free, leaving a hot trail down his cheek. James, little James, the thought pierced him.

The boy had come from absolute hell. His father had fallen from an unsecured scaffolding, leaving his spine completely ruined. Compensation was out of the question; not a single penny was granted. Since then, the family had woven straw braids at home, sewn linings into hats, and toiled at the corner shop—but the money was never enough. The old man took to drinking. First beer, then cheap gin. The more he drank, the more he beat the family, and the beatings grew bloodier. He didn’t just thrash James, but the mother too, until she fled to the neighbors, severely injured.

Clark had gotten James out of there—finally, after an unspeakable struggle with the authorities. The younger siblings went to foster families, while James was put in a council room in the south. It was supposed to be his salvation. Then came that icy winter night. Frost had formed on the window, so the heater burned all through the night. The flue of the old gas fire, however, blocked. The next morning, James was stiff. "Carbon monoxide poisoning," the district medical officer concluded.

After that, Clark was broken.

His colleagues patted him on the shoulder: "An accident, Clark. You did everything you could." But to him, those were just empty words. His own self-reproach crushed him to the ground, robbed him of his nights, and ate away at his self-confidence. He could no longer bear the misery. He threw in his job. He wanted to be a social worker no longer. On a sudden impulse, he fled to Bedford. It was meant to be the start of a new life.

With a sharp clatter, Clark set the photo back down. He sipped from his coffee mug. The hot liquid burned his tongue, waking him up. The corners of his mouth twitched upward. He rubbed his chin, looking at the Remington.

"I solve people's problems," he said aloud. "But no longer as a social worker. From now on, as an author. With humor and self-help."

"Out with the old stories. Down to work," he egged himself on. He sank into the chair, set the mug down, and placed his fingertips on the round metal keys. His fingers took off, barely able to be held back. Outside on the construction site, the machinery hammered out the beat.

Clark typed about Luton. About the hunger that hollowed out the boys' cheeks. About the poverty that lay like grey mold upon the houses. The keys clattered rapidly. The paper filled up.

Suddenly, his fingers stopped. He froze mid-movement.

What is the point of all this? Ripping open old wounds? I am here to bury the past and start anew, he admonished himself.

Clark stared at the paper before him. His arrival in Bedford yesterday flashed before his mind's eye. The sergeant, his coldness. With a loud rip, Clark tore the sheet from the roller. He crumpled it up, the edges cutting into his palm. A single toss, and the paper flew through the open window into the street.

"Enough of that," he decided.

Anger burned in his gut. He thought of Aaron Baker. That overbearing bastard from the station. I'll show you, Clark thought, feeling the muscles in his jaw tighten.

A fresh sheet zipped into the machine. He pounded away at the metal. Faster than before, starting almost wildly. The typebars hammered revenge onto the paper:


The Imperial State Crime of Crumb Collaboration:

Arthur Pendelton was hungry. A permanent condition in the England of these days. Prime Minister Attlee’s economy of scarcity regulated everything, even the sparrows. Feeding wild birds was forbidden — a state crime. Wheat belonged to reconstruction, not to the birds along the Great Ouse River.

Arthur loitered on a damp park bench, his flat cap pulled low over his eyes. Inside his coat pocket lay the contraband: a rock-hard crust of National Bread — as dry as the bricks of the London Brick Company.

He glanced to the left. Then another glance to the right. The coast was clear.

He broke off a crumb, a tiny little piece. The morsel slipped into the grass. Instantly, a pigeon darted forward. It was fat, grey, downright subversive, and it began to peck.

"Halt! Ministry of Food!" Sergeant Baker bellowed, springing out from behind a boxwood bush. He held his notebook before him like a loaded Sten gun.

"Pendelton!" Baker hissed, grinning. "Poultry anarchy! Illegal carbohydrates! Waste of imperial property!"

"The wind, Sergeant!" Arthur stammered. His hands shook. "A meteorological accident!"

"The wind is coming from the east, Pendelton. The bread fell to the west. I have noted it all, make no mistake about it."
Clark paused. He let out a dry laugh. A faint echo bounced back from the walls. If the Bedfordshire Times printed this, Baker would be the laughingstock of the town. He changed the sergeant in the text to "Inspector Butterbee." Better safe than sorry , Clark decided. Swiftly, he signed it with a fictitious name, having absolutely no desire to be paid a visit by the actual police.

He downed the lukewarm remains of his coffee, pulled the manuscript from the machine, and hurried out. "Off to the newspaper's submission box," he rejoiced.

On his way back, he passed Hartley’s on Midland Road.

Hartley’s — what a strange shop. There was no counter in the traditional sense, no shop assistants waiting behind the wood to fulfill the customers' wishes. Self-service was the experiment here. Inside, women wandered through the aisles with wire baskets. Some looked truly bewildered. They were looking for the counter where one would normally hand over the shopping list.

Clark reached into his pocket. The ration coupons allocated to him were damp with sweat and slightly grubby. He stared at the price board in the shop window. Someone had hastily scribbled on it in chalk. "This rationing is eating the country alive," Clark muttered. "It's worse than during the war." He scanned the board. What was he supposed to buy? A loaf of National Bread that tasted like grey paste? Or spend one shilling and two pence on gristly minced meat? For two ounces of butter and a single, measly egg, the points wouldn't stretch nearly far enough, he concluded in frustration.

Behind the glass, tins were stacked high. They formed pyramids of Spam. Beside them stood Snoek—a dreadful, fishy mackerel substitute from South Africa. The Ministry of Food praised it, but the public loathed it. On melting chunks of ice lay a dark slab of whale meat. The future of meat in Great Britain, the posters proclaimed. When fried, the stuff stank abominably of blubber, Clark remembered with disgust.

He swallowed his revulsion. Starvation or fish? He hadn't decided yet when he pushed down the latch. The brass bell above the door struck, emitting a sharp, bright chime. At that exact moment, a woman brushed past him onto the pavement. It wasn't just any woman; she was an apparition. Grey, post-war Bedford was instantly forgotten. Clark could not tear his eyes away from her. She wore a tailored suit. It was undoubtedly pre-war quality, but it looked immaculate, and her hat sat jauntily on her brow. All around, the common folk shuffled by in patched coats, yet this lady exuded expensive French perfume. Black market goods, no doubt, he thought — but even then, almost priceless.

Clark forgot to breathe.

Her heels clacked against the potholes. She crossed the street, slipping between two smoking double-decker buses and a sputtering motorcycle. The crowd swallowed her up. She was gone.

Clark gawked after her. Who was that? he asked himself, entirely entranced.

His hunger vanished. The price board had lost all interest. He rushed back out into the street, leaving the wire basket behind and ignoring the tins of Snoek. A minute later, he returned to the shop, heading straight for the girl at the till.

"Miss," he gasped. His hand was on his chest, his breath shallow. "The lady just now... Who on earth was that? Some relation of the Duke's?"

The shop assistant was just cutting a coupon out of a ration book. She looked up, tucked a stray lock under her hairnet, and smirked. "Out of your league, mate. Handsome eyes won't help you there."

"You know her?" He leaned over the counter, tapping his foot impatiently.

"Everyone knows her. That's Eyleen Newland. Former musical star from London's West End. In the twenties, the capital was at her feet. They were breaking down the doors for her at the Palace Theatre." The cashier shrugged. "Here in Bedford, that much class just stands out among all the coal dust."

"Eyleen Newland!" Clark lied. He snapped his fingers, playing the connoisseur. "Of course! The West End. How could I forget a name like that. She lives here in Bedford?"

"Yes, for a while now—at least since her husband passed. She lives right here on Midland Road. See that faded sign over there?" She pointed through the window. "Hebblethwaite’s Toys—that's where she stays. Right above the toy shop. I see her sitting by the window there sometimes."

Clark nodded, tipped his forehead in thanks, and hurried out of the shop. He returned home as if in a trance.
