Chapter 16:


Read the text aloud:



The impact was hard. A shooting pain seared up Clark’s left arm as his shoulder struck the rough pavement of the alley. He pressed his lips together, stifling a gasp. Not a sound! Above him, through the open window, he could hear the creaking of floorboards. Then came the dull thud of a heavy body sinking into a chair. Sergeant Baker was back at his desk.

Clark lay motionless. The cold cobblestones pressed against his cheek. From the narrow opening above drifted a sweet, familiar scent—a heavy, damp note of tobacco, the kind common in Bedford.

Clark lay motionless. The cold cobblestones pressed against his cheek. From the narrow opening above drifted a sweet, familiar scent — a heavy, damp note of tobacco, the kind common in Bedford. High time to clear out,Clark decided.

On all fours, he crawled along the damp brick wall until the alley opened up into St Paul’s Square. He pulled himself up, brushed the dirt from his jacket, and forced himself to adopt a firm, confident stride. The main thing now was to blend in completely. He retrieved his bicycle from the alcove, swung himself onto the saddle, and dissolved into the stream of factory workers walking silently toward their evening freedom.

Even as he pedalled, a question plagued him: Who besides Richard could possibly make sense of this note? No one, he decided. But Richard would know the stuff; of that he was certain. For years, the man had ingested every chemical creation available just to numb the memories of the war. Richard knew what lay hidden in the poison cabinets of the laboratories.

He had to get back to Luton. It was a thirty-kilometre journey each way. With his mangled shoulder, it was a murderous distance. Every time he pushed down on the pedals and his shoulder shifted forward, agony flared through his torso. His arm hung heavy and useless on the handlebars. The wind lashed against his face, while the grey sludge of the country road splattered against his shoes. He made poor progress, the bicycle wobbling slightly. Time was slipping through his fingers.

By the time he reached the dilapidated street in Luton, Richard was already standing in the open doorway of his flat. His hands were shoved in his pockets, and he was about to greet Clark with a rough slap to the upper arm. But he checked himself, noticing the involuntary twitching in Clark’s face and the limp arm.

“Bloody hell, Clark, what have you done?” he called out, his gruff voice laced with clear concern.

Clark simply let the bicycle drop onto the pavement. The metal clattered against the asphalt. Exhausted to the bone, he stumbled toward the steps. Richard grabbed him by his uninjured arm to steady him. “Let’s get you inside first.” Together they entered the house.

Once in the living room, they collapsed straight onto the sofa. Clark did not waste time with pleasantries. His trembling fingers dug beneath his damp shirt and pulled out the crumpled note. The paper was sticky with sweat.

“Richard, what is this used for?” He slid the slip of paper across the stained wooden table.

Richard glanced down at it, inspecting the writing suspiciously. A brief, incredulous laugh escaped him. “Sodium Amytal? What on earth is this about?”

“Please, Richard.” Clark’s voice was nothing more than a raspy plea. He clutched his aching shoulder. “Just tell me what it does.”

Richard leaned back, his eyes narrowing. “Well, if I were to slip this stuff into your tea, you’d become so talkative you wouldn’t believe it. Even in small doses, it strips away every inhibition. An unbelievable sense of relaxation washes over you. You lose total control over what you say. Completely uninhibited.”

“And if someone takes it every four hours?” Clark looked his friend dead in the eye, rubbing his upper arm.

“Every four hours?” Richard stared at him in disbelief, his pupils dilated. “Who the hell would do that? Not even the frontline military doctors dared to use it continuously. They injected it into traumatised soldiers to break through psychological blocks. To get rid of shell shock. But not on a relentless loop!”

“And if the boy they’re giving it to isn’t sick at all? What would they need it for then?” Clark pressed on. He leaned forward, locking his gaze onto Richard.

“How should I know? To aid sleep, as a heavy sedative for severe anxiety disorders. To keep someone quiet.” Richard struck the table with the flat of his hand. “Where did you get this bloody note, Clark? I need to know what’s going on.”

“It was in Edgar Tyler’s interrogation file.”

“In the file?” Richard paused. He brought his hand to his mouth, biting hard on his thumb. He was thinking. The gears in his mind were visibly turning. “Are you suspecting what I’m suspecting?” A horrified look met Clark. “With that stuff, they break your cognitive control. The patient becomes a puppet. Incredibly suggestible. Impressionable to the point of collapse.” He clicked his tongue—a nasty sound. “Is that what they’ve done?”

“Richard, nothing about these interrogations makes sense.” Clark’s voice was nearly cracking. “Why did they go into his cell time and time again? Why did the Sergeant show up every single time with that godforsaken court psychiatrist in tow? The whole thing stinks to high heaven!”

“Indeed,” Richard murmured, staring at the paper. “It is all highly peculiar.”

A long silence stretched through the room. Only the ticking of the wall clock could be heard.

“Tell me,” Richard broke the silence, his voice now cuttingly serious, “did Edgar tell you anything else? About the interrogation itself? Did they mention syringes?”

“No. Unfortunately not.” Clark looked up at the clock; the brief glance burned his exhausted eyes. “It’s past eight. Visiting hours at the prison are over. And the trial starts tomorrow morning at nine.” A heavy groan escaped his chest.

Richard shook his head. “You’re not cycling back tonight. Not in this state.” Clark was to stay on the sofa to save his strength for what awaited them tomorrow at Shire Hall.

The night became a war of attrition.

Time and again Clark jolted awake as nightmares clawed at his mind. He saw Edgar, trapped in the wooden dock. But there was no cross-examination. The judge, whose scarlet robes billowed like blood, was already climbing onto the railing at the opening of the session. In his hands, he held a thick, coarse hemp rope. He tied it to the wrought-iron chandelier and looked down at the boy. “Put your head through the noose, Tyler!” thundered through the courtroom.

“Stop it!” Clark screamed at the top of his lungs. His voice echoed off the bare walls of the living room. “Can’t you see he’s innocent? He didn’t do it!”

Suddenly, he felt a hand on his forehead. Was it the judge? Was he going to hang him from the chandelier next?

Drenched in sweat, Clark snapped his eyes open. The dim morning light cut through the curtains. It was only Richard. He held a wet, cold cloth, pressing it gently against Clark’s burning temples.

“Easy now, old friend,” he said softly. He sat on the edge of the sofa for a while longer, silently smoking a cigarette until Clark’s breathing grew shallower. Only then did he return to his room.

But the shadows remained.

The moment Clark’s eyes closed again, McAllroy appeared before him—the old ghost from Bedford, his face twisted in contempt. “First you drove poor James to his death, and now you’re abandoning Edgar,” the figure hissed. “What a failure you are. And you call yourself a social worker. Pathhetic.” McAllroy spat on the floor. A wet patch formed on the carpet. “We have no use for your sort in our beautiful Bedford.”

The scene shifted once more; his mind could find no peace.

He found himself back in Baker’s office. The scent of oak and tobacco filled his nostrils. He heard the rattling of keys right outside the door. The Sergeant was here! Clark wanted to shut the rummaged wardrobe, but the hem of his jacket was caught. He tugged at it in vain — the wood wouldn't budge. In a panic, he yanked the wardrobe door open once more.

And there it hung: the light-coloured duffle coat. It stood out against the uniforms, seeming to absorb the sparse light like a burning charge of phosphorus.

It all happened in a flash. Clark stepped back, tore the fabric free, and slammed the wardrobe door shut with all his might. A loud crash echoed through the room. He bolted upright, his heart in his throat.

But there had been no crash. There was only the silence of Richard’s living room. Here on the sofa, he was safe. Or was he?

Clark stared at the ceiling as the images of the dream bled into his waking mind. The duffle coat. That damned, light-coloured duffle coat. He hadn’t just spotted it in the wardrobe. He had seen it before. His thoughts raced, hitting him like a physical blow. Of course, those coats were fashionable; every second man in Bedford wore one. But there was that one detail. The photographs, the realization flashed through Clark. The hooded man in the surveillance shots — that very fellow had been wearing exactly such a coat.

In an instant, Clark sat bolt upright. The blanket slipped to the floor. Was it possible? Could Sergeant Aaron Baker himself be the man beneath the hood? If that were true, the entire trial wasn't a mistake. It was a systematic execution.

Clark leapt to his feet. He barely felt his shoulder anymore; the adrenaline had completely flushed the pain away. He began pacing nervously around the room, the old floorboards creaking beneath his bare feet. He had to go back. He stole a quick glance at the clock. It was just after six in the morning. If he got on his bicycle right now and pedalled with every ounce of breath in his lungs, he would be in Bedford by eight. One hour before the trial began. One hour to stop the court.
