Chapter 4


Read the text aloud:



Clark threw his arms up and stretched his tendons. The vertebrae in his back cracked softly—a liberating sound after hours of stiffness.

He took a deep breath. A rare, golden dream still hung like a gentle veil in his mind. Everything within it had possessed a somnambulistic clarity: Eyleen Tyler, his Eyleen. She was standing by the mailbox, her fingers trembling with anticipation as she tore open his envelope. In the dream, he had seen her eyes grow misty. A heavy, redeeming tear had fallen onto the rough paper. The material absorbed the moisture, tightening and forming a tiny, rippled indentation. In the memory, Clark felt a ravenous longing to drink that moisture from the page with his lips. He didn't just want her tears—he wanted to absorb this girl entirely. Every single inch. She was no ordinary human being to him. She was a gift placed on this earth by God himself, a turning point on legs.

He pressed the heels of his hands firmly into his eye sockets. Black dots danced before his inner eye as he opened his lids once more. Reality demanded its due.

Then, there was silence.

Clark paused. He held his breath to hear better. Nothing—not a single sound drifted through the open window. On the nearby construction site, the otherwise deafening noise of the jackhammers was silent. No roaring car engines over at the intersection, no rattling milk bottles, not a single barking dog. His eyes wandered to the wall clock. The hands stood at just past half nine. At this hour, the neighborhood normally pulsated like a startled anthill. "Was today Sunday? No," he shook his head, mentally reviewing the week. "It was Tuesday—the Tuesday!" he snapped back to reality.

The realization hit him like an electric shock. He lunged two steps forward and leaned over the wooden windowsill. Ashburnham Road was barely recognizable. Flags were flying everywhere.

Strings of bunting stretched from house wall to house wall, forming a colorful roof of fabric. The iron streetlamps were barely visible; the residents had wrapped them meters deep in red, blue, and white ribbons. Even the dusty crowns of the plane trees shone with color. Hand-sized paper flowers in the national colors were tucked between the leaves, carefully knotted into the branches.

Clark whirled around. He rushed to the wardrobe and flung the doors open. His fingers groped in the dark interior until they touched the cool wood of a flagpole. He pulled it out, along with the heavy, folded Union Jack. Today was no day to be an outsider. Today, conformity meant pride.

He cursed softly, as his window had no bracket on the outside. "So where to put the wooden shaft?" he asked himself in a panic. His gaze swept through the stark room. "The chair!" flashed through his mind.

He grabbed the backrest and dragged the piece of furniture across the floorboards with a loud squeak, right in front of the window. "That’s not enough," he noted, dismayed. "The weight of the flag will drag the chair outside." He lunged for the heavy oak table. With all his might, he shoved the edge of the table against the seat, wedging the wood firmly until nothing moved. Then, with a forceful swing, he jammed the pole through the wide gap in the backrest. The wooden knob struck the tabletop—everything was secure now. He looked outside. The Union Jack unfolded with a loud snap in the wind, casting heavy, rhythmic waves over the pavement.

"A vassal," Clark felt, "that was exactly what he was. A loyal servant to his queen." Pride swelled his chest as he watched the fluttering fabric.

Shaving took twice as long this morning. His hand guided the razor over his cheeks in millimeter-short, meticulously precise strokes. He felt the sharp blade against his skin, cutting away every last, tiny hair on his chin. He wanted to be flawless. Once for the young woman on the throne, and once for Eyleen, the ruler of his thoughts. Afterward, he reached for the blue bottle. He splashed a generous amount of Old Spice into his palms, rubbed them together, and pressed it onto his burning skin. A dense, spicy cloud of fragrance enveloped him. Finally, his hair: he squeezed a dab of Brylcreem from the tube. The cream smelled clean. He smoothed it back with his palms and drew a razor-sharp, deep parting on the left side with his horn comb. His hair gleamed wet, yet lay heavy and pliable against his head without becoming sticky. He slipped into the dark double-breasted suit. It was his best suit. The heavy fabric stretched comfortably across his shoulders, making his chest look broader. His fingertips wandered over the rough tweed of the sleeve, while the pure silk of the lining offered a cool sensation from within.

He left the house and walked briskly toward Midland Road. Here, on the city's main artery, a completely different picture presented itself. The silence of the side streets was gone. People stood tightly packed, shoulder to shoulder. Real crowds had formed in front of the display windows of the few electronics shops, spilling out onto the roadway. Clark headed toward the mass stalling in front of the shop. He stood on his tiptoes. She had to be around here somewhere. Pushing past an elderly couple with a murmured apology, he made his way through. At the very front, right against the glass pane, a few old men had settled onto wooden kitchen chairs they had brought from home. No one spoke. All eyes stared at the small, flickering screen in the shop window.

On the gray screen, the majestic state coach was moving. It all looked surreal. Inside sat Elizabeth II. She appeared delicate, almost lost on the wide, upholstered bench. In her youth, a mere twenty-seven years old, she possessed a near-flawless beauty. The crowd on Midland Road exhaled deeply, as if with one breath. A loud cheer erupted as the carriage left the palace gates behind. On the television, it was a fairy tale in black and white. The dress of white satin silk gleamed despite the poor picture quality of the tube television. One could sense the heavy grandeur of the countless gold and silver threads woven in floral patterns across the fabric. Thousands of tiny pearls caught the light of the studio lamps. In three hours, this young woman would bear the destiny of the empire.

Next to Clark stood a corpulent woman in a floral dress. Her breathing was heavy. She fumbled hastily in her bag, pulled out a handkerchief with lace trim, and dabbed the damp corners of her eyes dry. Immediately after, she raised her arm and waved euphorically at the glass screen, as if she were standing directly at the roadside in Westminster.

The general frenzy seized Clark as well. A powerful throb struck his chest from within. His heart hammered against his ribs as the carriage pulled up outside Westminster Abbey and the Queen disappeared into the dark portal.

Suddenly, a shift rippled through the spectators.

The old men in the front row leaned on their crutches and walking sticks, laboriously hoisting themselves up. No one remained seated. The crowd closed ranks. As if on command, the right hands of the spectators rose and pressed flat against their chests, right where their hearts beat. Then the voices chimed in. Raw, loud, and unrehearsed, the coronation anthem echoed through Midland Road:

I was glad when they said unto me: We will go into the house of the Lord...

The singing swelled, full of fervor. Clark joined in; his deep voice mingling with the soprano of the women.

Vivat! Vivat Regina Elizabetha! Vivat! Vivat! Vivat!

The Latin cries echoed off the house walls. As the final note died away and the front rows slowly lowered themselves back onto their chairs, Clark’s eyes searched the surroundings.

"There she was," he noted with relief.

At the left fringe of the crowd, right next to the front row of chairs, Eyleen stood with Liz. Clark felt the saliva dry up in his mouth. He swallowed with difficulty. Eyleen wore a dark blue dress, its neckline adorned with small, gleaming pearls. She looked like a sovereign herself, waiting for her coronation. He locked his eyes onto her face and began to push his way ruthlessly through the throng, leading with his shoulders. He kept his gaze fixed rigidly on her.

But Eyleen wasn't looking at the television.

She was gazing intently toward the opposite side of the street. Clark followed the line of her eyes. A sudden, icy shiver ran down his spine, making the fine hairs on his forearms stand on end. On the other side stood Sergeant Baker. His police uniform was impeccably pressed, the silver buttons reflecting the dull daylight. Baker undoubtedly possessed that dark, magnetic charisma that men like Clark despised. His angular, oval face looked stern, almost cruel. Clark felt the memory boil up inside him. The incident with the little Italian boy on the corner — Baker had shown no mercy. A sadistic fellow in the civil service. Clark averted his eyes and looked back over at Eyleen.

He froze once more.

Eyleen had arched her dark eyebrows. A subtle, knowing smile played upon her lips. She cast a quick, coquettish glance at the sergeant. At the same time, she raised her hand and, with a slow, provocative motion, stroked the fabric of her skirt. She was flirting. Unquestionably, she was ingratiating herself with this man. Clark squeezed his eyelids shut, hoping the image would vanish. But it remained. A hot wave of bitter envy rose from the pit of his stomach, sharp as acid. "A rival. And of all people, that man," he groaned quietly to himself.

On the screen, the Queen was just taking her oath. The crowd around Clark clapped enthusiastically. In this moment of commotion, Eyleen seized the favor of the second. She slipped deftly through the gaps in the applauding spectators, step by step, over toward Baker. By the time the television switched to the anointing and the image of the Queen disappeared behind the silk canopy, Eyleen was already standing directly in front of the policeman.

Clark watched it all with deep disdain.

On the television, the Queen was removing her royal robe of state. On the street, Eyleen did something similar. She reached for the hem of her long, white fabric gloves. Slowly, she drew them off, finger by finger, exposing her skin. Then she extended her bare hand to Baker. The sergeant took it. They shook hands—for far too long, it seemed to Clark. The leather of Baker’s glove chafed against her skin. Clark felt a throbbing in his temples. He had to intervene. But a direct confrontation with a lawman on the open street was out of the question. His knees were too weak for that. He needed another way. Clark thought hard. Then the solution hit him: Liz!

He turned and pushed in the opposite direction, back to where Liz still lingered alone by the shop window. It took agonizingly long minutes to fight his way through the wall of backs and elbows. Then he slipped in beside her.

"Just look at that magnificent gown!" Liz exclaimed without taking her eyes off the screen as she noticed Clark’s presence. Her voice was full of excitement. "Do you have any idea how much work goes into a hem like that? At least twenty seamstresses must have worked on it day and night."

Clark forced a slight smile, though his jaw tensed. "If you say so, Miss Tyler." He nodded. "You would know best. A professional dressmaker sees that sort of thing instantly."

Liz turned her face away. A distinct, deep crimson rose from her collarbones up into her cheeks. "Oh, stop it. I'm really not all that talented."

"Nonsense," Clark insisted, lowering his voice to sound more confidential. "The dress your mother is wearing today — that was conjured up entirely by your own hands, wasn't it, or am I mistaken?"

Liz looked down. Her fingers kneaded the hem of her own jacket. "It suits her well, don't you think? I was so afraid. I really thought I’d botch the cut at the shoulders."

"It is flawless," Clark confirmed, his gaze already stealing over the heads of the crowd again, squinting toward Eyleen. "Absolutely perfect. No tailor on Savile Row could have done it better. And it fits like a glove. If it weren't Coronation Day, I’d roundly declare your mother to be the true Queen of London."

Liz fiddled with the lace of her blouse. Her fingers plucked restlessly at the fabric.

"Tell me..." Clark lowered his voice noticeably, his eyes drawn back to the other side of the street like a magnet, "is there actually something going on between your mother and the sergeant?"

"No. Really, no." Liz shook her head. She had long since noticed the starved look on Clark’s face. A spark of pity lay in her eyes. "Aaron Baker is simply the best sergeant this city has ever had. He helps wherever he can. Always friendly. Always courteous. Everyone in the neighborhood loves him."

"Everyone," echoed inside Clark. What an ugly word.

He peered over again. Across the pavement, Eyleen’s hand was now wandering upward. Her fingertips touched the dark fabric of Baker’s uniform jacket and slid slowly down his sleeve—an open invitation. Clark held his breath. Baker, however, did not react as hoped. He turned around, his boots crunching on the gravel. Right beside him, a little boy who barely reached the adults' hips was jostling for space. Baker bent down. His broad hands caught the child under the arms, hoisting him up with a single, effortless motion and setting him on his shoulders.

"There you go, you'll see more this way," the policeman’s deep, calm voice drifted through the murmuring crowd.

The child squealed with delight. In his little fist, he held a thick, already precariously tilted waffle cone with vanilla ice cream. It did not escape Clark: for a fraction of a second, Eyleen’s features slipped. Her seductive allure vanished, but she quickly caught herself. Her laugh sounded bright and carefree again at once as she beamed at the boy and the sergeant once more.

When a sudden murmur rippled through Midland Road, Clark tore his gaze abruptly away from the street scene and snapped back to the shop window.

The television flickered in stark contrast. On the screen, the centuries-old ritual was unfolding: the spurs, the heavy Sword of State, the glittering armlets. One by one, the regalia of power were placed into the hands of the young Queen, followed by the ring and the scepter. And then came the decisive moment: the Archbishop raised the heavy St Edward’s Crown with both hands. He paused. He brought it down.

At that exact solemn moment, as the crowd held its breath, disaster struck over on the pavement. A thick, sticky drop of yellowish vanilla ice cream detached itself from the boy’s waffle. It fell straight through the air and splashed precisely onto Eyleen’s left shoulder. In all the commotion, she didn't even notice; her eyes were glued curiously to the screen.

Only when the ceremony was over and the newly crowned Queen Elizabeth II had left Westminster Abbey to the swelling notes of the organ did the tension among the spectators dissolve. The crowds began to move. Eyleen turned around and stalked back toward Liz and Clark with long, sweeping strides.

"Oh, Mother..." Liz grimaced, her eyes widening in alarm. "What on earth happened?"

"Happened?" Eyleen’s voice pitched artificially high. She put on her most innocent look. In her mind, a single thought was pounding: she believed Liz had noticed how icily Aaron Baker had just brushed her off. The humiliation burned on her cheeks.

"Your sleeve!" Liz whispered hastily into her ear, almost frantic. "It's covered in yellow stains!"

Eyleen froze. Swiftly, she snapped her head to the left. Her gaze fell upon the fabric. Only now did she comprehend what a sticky mess the strange toddler had made of her clothing. The noble, painstakingly tailored dress was ruined—a total loss of melted sugar. An uncontrollable, hot fury rose within Eyleen, rushing straight to her temples. She wanted to scream, but hundreds of people stood all around her. She swallowed her wrath until her throat ached.

Hastily, her hand dashed into the deep pocket of her skirt. She fumbled for a handkerchief to contain the damage. Her fingers caught the fabric, but at that very moment, she pulled something else out along with it: a white slip of paper.

The paper drifted slowly to the ground before her, coming to rest on the dirty asphalt. Eyleen stared down at it, unable to react. Her thoughts were instantly blocked.

Clark, however, did not hesitate. He lunged forward, bent low, and fished the paper out of the dust with two fingers. Straightening up, he smoothed the edges and handed it back to her with a slow, almost solemn gesture. As he did, he sought her gaze, pressing his eyes into hers. "I truly hope it is my poem," he said in a gentle voice, offering a charming smile.

"Poem? What poem, if you please?" Eyleen muttered, beside herself. She impatiently unfolded the paper, only to see the very lines she had cursed and mocked the evening before.

There was no escape now. Liz had recognized the text as well. She looked from the note to Clark and back again. Eyleen's cheeks glowed with rage.

"Yes... your poem," Eyleen finally squeezed out. Her smile looked as if it were stapled to her face. "I... I happened to have it on me."

"So you liked it!" Clark’s face lit up. He took a step toward her. "I knew it. I knew it would touch your heart." Once more, his gaze bored deep into her eyes — demanding, almost possessive.

Eyleen could not withstand the pressure. She turned away abruptly. "Liz, we are going home now. It has been a long day. Enough for today." She took a resolute step toward her daughter.

Liz reacted quickly. She tugged at a fine, lightweight scarf loosely draped around the collar of her own blouse. With a swift movement, she untied the fabric and handed it to her mother. Eyleen accepted the cloth without a shred of thanks and threw it over her left shoulder with an elegant motion. The light fabric fell perfectly over the ugly stain. The embarrassment was concealed, her dignity salvaged.

Eyleen took a deep, audible breath. Then she grabbed Liz by the arm, pulled the girl tightly to her side, and pressed away. The heels of her shoes clacked hastily on the pavement—away from the electronics shop, away from Clark.

"See you soon, my love!" Clark called out after them. His voice nearly cracked with euphoria. He cupped his hands around his mouth like a megaphone. "Read the newspaper tomorrow! I am going to publish a major article about today! I will give you a special mention! Count on it!"

The two women did not turn around again. Their silhouettes slowly vanished into the stream of celebrating people. Clark was left behind alone. He closed his eyes and drew the air deep into his nose. There it still was, the fleeting, sweet scent of her perfume, lingering like an invisible trail in the warm afternoon air. He inhaled the last remnants of it, storing it in his memory, and turned around. The walk home awaited.

Back home, Clark’s fingers were still trembling so violently with excitement that he missed the keyhole three times before the bit finally bit. A loud click echoed, then he threw the door open and breathed in the musty air of his garret.

She had read it, his poem!

This knowledge almost consumed him from the inside out. Eyleen hadn't just skimmed his lines — she had carried the folded paper close to her person, hidden in the fabric of her dress, while outside the whole world celebrated the coronation of the young Queen. Clark stood in the middle of the room, unable even to take off his suit jacket. His hand pressed flat against his chest; beneath his ribs, his heart hammered like a caged bird against its bars.

He closed his eyes. Instantly, Midland Road was back. The glare of the June day. The smell of damp asphalt, perfume, and banners made of cheap crepe paper. Thousands of people crowded against the barricades, yet reality blurred. There was only her. He saw again how Eyleen lowered her eyelids, how her lips formed into that one, promising smile. A moment ago, he had believed those glances were meant for the sergeant in his stiff uniform.

"A mistake. A wonderful mistake," he laughed out loud and began to dance. She had looked at him. At him, Clark! Every glance, every nuance of that fleeting movement of her hand in the rustle of the crowd had been a secret message, which now flowed through his veins like a warm current. He stopped moving. Paradise smelled of linoleum and cheap tea.

"Hey! Make way there!" A shrill laugh cut through the wall. Outside on the pavement, youths were bawling, throwing an empty bottle against a wall, which shattered with an ugly crash.

Clark startled. The hallucination popped.

"Were those his boys?" flashed through his mind. He took two quick steps to the window, which was still wide open, and looked down onto the street. The coldness of reality slapped him in the face. No, this wasn't Luton. He was no longer standing in the dusty backyard of the youth center, trying to soothe the anger of adolescent factory workers. Clark the social worker no longer existed—today, he was a man on a mission.

A deep, almost painful sense of satisfaction spread within him. He reached for the wooden flagpole he had hung out the window to mark the occasion and yanked the colorful fabric back into the room with a sharp tug. He slid the table back against the wall, the wobbly chair following. Then he slammed the window shut. The noise of the street was locked out. Finally, silence reigned.

Clark stepped up to the oak shelves. His palms were damp as he gripped the heavy, black Remington by its cast-iron sides. He lifted the typewriter, set it down with a dull thud right in the center of the tabletop, and cranked a fresh, crisp sheet of paper under the platen. The typebars waited. His fingers hovered over the keys. He had to capture the heat of this day before it slipped away.

The first letter bit into the white page, black and final:


To the Phantom of the Front Row (A Royal Peace Offering)

Composed for the Coronation Day Supplement of the "Bedfordshire Times," June 1953

All London was dizzy with bunting, Westminster Abbey shook with pride,
As yesterday our youthful Queen sought the throne of the world wide.
The Archbishop bore pounds of gold, the crown jewels flashed and gleamed,
Yet I saw only your countenance as through the crushing crowd you streamed!
What is that grand imperial spectacle of the night,
When your stroll down Midland Road has made my heart burst with delight?
You stride like a Broadway star, your lashes elegantly curled,
While I — a simple man of thirty-six boys in this world —
Stand at the roadside like Woolworths’ cheapest mannequin in line,
And freeze in silence whenever I watch you paint your lips so fine.
I barely know you by sight, alas! From Hebblethwaite’s toy shop door,
You live above, I look up, plagued by love's aches forevermore.
The Queen received the Sovereign's Orb, the sceptre, and the grace,
How I longed to hear a single word in this bunting-strewn place!
My heart beats louder than the drums of the Palace Guard,
When you promenade in your tweed suit — top tier, a stellar sight unmarred.
Let us forget the rationing! What are bacon and sugar tokens now!
Let us park our minds today by the Great Ouse River's brow!
Allow the bold writer of this humble rhyme today
A rendezvous at the park café — secret, far from the office away?
I'll bring fresh cocoa, illicitly bought on the black market side,
For a single smile of yours, I have already nearly died.
Do not turn me down as you so often do with your cold stare,
The coronation is over, my sweet, the world seeks peace in the air.
Let us write history, here in Bedford Park so grand,
A royal introduction — I long for it so badly in this land!
He read his masterpiece. Time and again, his eyes flew across the text as he recited it aloud. His own voice echoed off the bare walls of the garret until the verses were perfectly set. If she had liked the first poem, she would love this one. Of that he was convinced.

He went to the wobbly wardrobe. In the top drawer, he rummaged through old papers until his fingers struck firm cardstock. The best envelope he owned—cream-white and expensive. He smoothed the sheets, folded the poem with millimeter precision, and slipped it inside very gently, as if dealing with paper-thin glass that might shatter under the coarse touch of his fingers.

In the bathroom, he lost track of time. He smoothed back his hair, again and again, long after the brush found no resistance left. Against her numbing beauty, he was a nobody, he knew that. But today, when asking for a first rendezvous, no flaw could cling to him. He brushed down the heavy wool fabric of his suit and cleaned the dull leather of his shoes. He slipped into them, took a deep breath, and left the room.

The walk to Midland Road was short—yet still too long for his hammering heart. At the roadside bloomed a few white sweet peas, hanging halfway over a cast-iron fence. He hesitated. His conscience flared with a sharp pang. Theft? drummed in his head. He looked around cautiously. No one was there; the streets were completely deserted. "If they're spilling out of the garden anyway, no one will notice," he muttered to dull his hollow feeling. With a snap, his fingers broke the delicate stems. Hastily, he shoved the blossoms beneath the fabric of his suit jacket, hiding them from the world, and quickened his pace.

He knew the house; the number was burned into his memory. He pressed the small brass button of the doorbell.

The buzzer sounded. The heavy front door yielded, revealing the dark stairwell. He climbed upward, step by step, to the second floor.

"Oh, it's you!" The voice was bright, striking him like a warm summer breeze. It was Liz. She stood in the open doorway, an apron tied around her waist, smiling at him.

Clark drew his hand out from under his jacket. His palm was wet, the white sweet peas already looking slightly bent. "Good evening, fair maiden." He attempted a smile, but his lips merely tightened rigidly. "Is your lady mother at home?"

"Please, come in," the girl said, her eyes shining. She clearly found this awkward suitor, standing in the hallway with flowers, rather touching.

He stepped over the threshold. Hardly had the door clicked shut behind him when a familiar voice cut through the apartment. It was sharp and crystal-clear.

"Liz? What is it? Who's there?"

Eyleen stepped into the hallway. She had shed the heavy suit of the day and now wore a loose evening gown of flowing silk fabric—cocktail-party elegance in the middle of Bedford. Clark swallowed; it was a dry, painful reflex. What a beauty, flashed through his mind. His limbs froze. He just stared at her. His princess.

"Yes?"

The word snapped him out of it. Eyleen's voice tolerated no daydreaming. "What is it you want now?" she challenged him, sharp and cold.

Clark fumbled for breath. He felt the courage drain from his fingertips. "Since we... today... in town..." he stammered, caught himself, and laboriously pushed the words forward. "At that event. It was so moving. As I said, I would capture everything in poetic words. After all, I am an author. So... my lady... may I recite my poem to you?"

Eyleen pressed her fingertips to her forehead. This man was simply insufferable. An impulse rippled through her posture—she wanted to throw him out on his ear. But then her eyes shifted. An amused glint flickered. She waved it off and gestured toward the drawing room: "Show him in, Liz."

She led the way and seated herself with majestic slowness in the large armchair. Liz retreated to the corner, taking a seat on a tiny stool. Beside her stood the wicker basket of knitting, overflowing with gray wool.

"Well then, what are we waiting for?" Eyleen prompted the guest. Her eyes pinned him like an insect.

Clark stepped forward. His fingers felt damp and sticky as he held out the white sweet peas to her. With his other hand, he solemnly drew the cream-white envelope from the inside pocket of his suit and unfolded the paper. Eyleen took the flowers; her fingers closing roughly around the delicate stems.

Clark took a deep breath. His voice trembled at the first verse, but then grew louder, carried by the yearning of his own lines. He did not look up. He failed to notice Liz quietly giggling to herself on her stool, delighted by the juxtaposition of the imagery: the grandeur of the Queen, the smallness of this man. Clark was blind to everything except his manuscript.

IBut inside Eyleen, things were boiling. Her lips pressed together, her hand clenched into a fist. The delicate blossoms of the sweet peas possessed no strength against the sudden pressure; the green sap of the stems oozed between her fingers as she crushed the flowers to the rhythm of his rhymes.

The final sentence died away.

From the stool in the corner came a brief, tentative patter of applause—a solitary sound that perished in the room like a falling stone. Afterward, an icy, heavy silence reigned.

Eyleen did not stir. Her gaze wandered slowly up Clark’s form: from the dusty tips of his shoes, across his brushed suit, and up to his flushed face. And back down again. She rose from the armchair, her dress rustling. She turned to her daughter.

"My dear," she said, her voice vibrating with a cutting, high-pitched elegance, "never in my life have I heard such a primitive creature say something so utterly devoid of class. What does this good-for-nothing think he is doing, stealing my time?"

She took a step toward Clark, yet looked right through him as if he were made of thin air. "No doubt he wants to ingratiate himself here to give his wretched writing career the polish it needs. Get this creature out of my sight. People like us do not associate with such low rabble. Show him out. He is never to show his face here again."

She turned toward the door.

"But yesterday!" Clark gasped. The word was a piercing cry of despair. He was trembling all over. "Yesterday, you actually liked my poem!"

Eyleen paused. She turned around slowly. A derisive smile played upon her lips. "Not even that," she replied coldly. "If you do not even understand countenance, what do you understand?" She tilted her chin and left the room. The door swung shut behind her.

The blow struck him like a thunderclap. Clark stood motionless, unable to breathe. Shame rose inside him like boiling water; he wanted the floor to split open and swallow him whole. He had not foreseen this brutality. His fingers went numb, and the envelope slipped from his grasp. The pages of paper drifted to the floor in slow, languid arcs, coming to rest upon the carpet.

His first tears broke free; he could not stop them. The water ran down his cheeks in hot trails and dripped onto his collar. Clark sank to his knees. Blindly, he fumbled for the scattered sheets on the floor, wanting only to gather them up and flee.

A hand rested upon his fingers. It was gentle and completely warm. He paused, looked up through the veil of tears, and saw Liz kneeling beside him. She pressed a clean fabric handkerchief into his hand and, with a gentle pressure against his elbow, helped him to his feet.

"Don't take it personally," she whispered hastily as she guided him toward the hallway. "My mother is not an easy person. She doesn't mean it so harshly. She has been a widow for quite some time now, and hasn't let anyone get close to her since. I'll take you to the door now."

She accompanied him down the stairs, step by step, all the way to the heavy front door of the tenement building. She spoke to him continuously, trying to soothe the pain and assuring him that the poem had brought her great joy.

Clark, however, did not register what she was saying. Her words were merely a dull roar in his ears, like a distant waterfall. His life energy, his grand emotions, the hope of the afternoon—everything had been extinguished in a single flash.

The front door closed behind him — a dry click.

Outside, he stood on the wet pavement. His feet refused to take the path back home; the garret was suddenly too cramped for him. He walked in the opposite direction. His steps were heavy, as if he were dragging iron chains behind him. Eventually, he sat on a damp bench in the city park. He was entirely alone. The chill of the evening crept through the fabric of his Sunday suit. Disillusioned, he stared into the void.


Upstairs in the apartment, crystal clinked.

Liz pushed open the door to the drawing room. Her mother was already standing by the sideboard, liqueur bottle in hand. Noticing the girl, she turned around. A shrill, throaty laugh burst from her lips.

"Is he finally gone?" she sneered, her eyes gleaming with amusement. "What a jokester! No, Liz, I was laughing so hard inside. How can anyone rhyme such nonsense together?" She poured the thick, sweet liqueur into a small glass and held the bottle invitingly in the air. "Would you like one too?"

"No, thank you," Liz said. Her voice was flat. She felt a dull chill in the pit of her stomach that had nothing to do with the temperature in the room; she could not share her mother's mirth.

"But I had a whale of a time seeing him suffer like that," Eyleen chortled on. She shook with laughter and tossed the liqueur down with a swift motion. "Did he really cry?" Her glass struck the tabletop with a hard thud. "The man is simply too good for this world. And naive! God, is he naive!" She wiped her cheek as if she had to brush away tears of amusement from her eyes. "Who does he think a great actress takes as a life partner? Certainly not an insignificant little nobody like him!"

Liz did not reply. Without a word, she walked to her stool in the corner, sat down, and pulled the wicker basket toward her. Her fingers reached for the knitting needles, letting the metal clack loudly against each other. It cut her to the quick to hear her mother speak this way. Every laugh from Eyleen’s mouth sliced like a knife through the silence of the room.


Meanwhile, in the park, a cold rain ate through the wool of Clark's suit. He raised his head, blinking against the drops, and looked down at himself. His Sunday shoes were dark lumps of wet leather. He set off at a brisk pace, so that the park was soon left behind him.

What on earth had he done wrong? he asked himself incessantly. Everything inside his chest tightened, yet a stubborn spark resisted. She couldn't have meant it that way. Impossible—it was all just a facade. Surely she just wanted him to fight for her even harder. He loved her, and she would love him too; they just needed to get to know each other properly. Women like Eyleen simply needed time.

His footsteps struck hard against the asphalt, and suddenly he remembered an essay he had read with surprise back in Luton. The piece had been titled Dr. Sargants' theories on "Pavlovian collapse through sensory overload". It had actually been a dense text from a medical journal. Since Clark had lacked the money for a psychology degree, he had devoured specialized literature and had been grateful for every article. Libraries had eventually become his home. Often he had still been sitting in the reading room at ten o'clock in the evening, until the warden turned the key in the lock with a polite, "Sorry, we’re closing."

Sargant’s approach had been radically new, Clark recalled. It was about wearing the other person down through a barrage of stimuli until their resistance collapsed — until the target agreed to a project they might have previously even detested.

Clark stopped beneath a streetlamp. The water ran from his hat down the back of his neck. Couldn't that work here too? he pondered.

A guilty conscience flared up. He wanted to protect Eyleen, not torment her. But sensory overload for a single rendezvous? Just to open her eyes? His thoughts raced. An inner war between morality and the naked greed for happiness had been ignited within him. Where did decency end? Where did the battle for the love of his life begin?
