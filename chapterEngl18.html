Chapter 18:


Read the text aloud:



Clark quickened his pace across the tiled floor. Then he paused for a brief moment, drawing the corridor’s stale air deep into his lungs, pressing his arms tight against his body, and forcing himself to think of Richard. Arrogant, loud, untouchable — that was how he needed to be right now.

“We need to talk,” Clark said, his tone brooking no argument as he stepped up to the sergeant, who was still perched on the bench, waiting to be called into the courtroom. He locked onto Baker’s pupils, keeping his gaze as rigid as a bird of prey. Digging his fingers into his suit pocket, Clark pulled out the crumpled paper covered in black ink and thrust it right under the sergeant’s nose. Only for a second, before letting it vanish back into his palm.

“With me,” he told Baker, leaving no room for misunderstanding. It was a command, not a request.

Clark turned on his heel and headed toward the wooden door of the men’s restroom at the end of the hallway. He could feel his own violent heartbeat in the back of his neck. He had almost lost his composure; the painstakingly maintained facade had almost collapsed when the steady scraping of heavy boot soles sounded behind him. Baker was actually following. A thrill of triumph coursed through Clark. He pressed down the heavy handle, stepped aside, and extended his arm invitingly. “After you, Mr. Baker.”

Baker stepped inside. Clark followed him. The latch of the men’s room clicked shut with a metallic snap. Then, dead silence.

Only the monotonous, distant hum of a broken fluorescent tube vibrated in the air. The room smelled pungent—of urinal cakes, wet concrete, and stale tobacco. Baker shifted his weight from one foot to the other, his leather shoes creaking loudly. The cramped space seemed to suffocate him.

“Where did you get that note?” Baker snapped at Clark. He took a heavy step forward, his chest puffed out, his brow furrowed deep.

Clark felt his stomach tighten painfully. Cold sweat broke out on the back of his neck. But suddenly, Richard’s raspy voice echoed in his head, repeating that one eternal sentence: “Show them who’s boss!”

Clark didn't back down an inch. He squared his shoulders, deliberately took his time slipping the paper back into his pocket, and looked down at the sergeant with a mocking gaze. “Well, look at that. The sergeant recognizes the note. What a surprise.”

“It’s not a surprise!” Baker yelled. Flecks of spit flew from his mouth as he gestured wildly. “You stole it. From my office! Stole it, yes, damn it, stole it! You’re a common thief, and you belong in front of a judge just like your hypocritical client!”

Clark offered a faint smile. Otherwise, he showed no reaction. Then, he slowly brushed an imaginary lint fiber from his sleeve to project a calculated coldness that he barely felt inside. “So it came from your office. Perhaps straight out of Edgar Tyler’s case file?”

He fell silent. The sentence hung between the damp tiles like heavy smoke.

Baker’s jaw muscles ground together. The coarse face lost some of its color as his eyes frantically searched Clark’s unmoving expression. The absolute stillness of his opponent seemed to unnerve him. Where was the trap? Why wasn't this social worker buckling?

“What do you want?” Baker spat out. The aggressiveness had given way to a gruff defiance.

“I want you to drop the case against Edgar Tyler. Immediately.” Clark’s voice possessed the temperature of liquid nitrogen.

Baker let out a short, hollow laugh, but his fingers wandered nervously to his top shirt button, which suddenly felt too tight. “Why on earth would I do that?”

“You know just as well as I do that something here stinks to high heaven.” Clark stepped closer until he could smell the other man’s sour breath. “This sudden, airtight confession after you and that shady forensic psychiatrist grilled the poor guy for hours — you don’t even believe it yourself.” He held the sergeant’s gaze. Seconds ticked by, stretched like rubber. “Where is the guy anyway? Shouldn’t the fine expert witness be sitting on the stand with you at a trial like this?”

Baker looked down at the floor tiles. His right hand fiddled with his belt. “He… he had an urgent prior engagement today… and since Edgar confessed anyway…”

“Rubbish!” Clark cut him off. “He’s not here because this whole thing is filthy. The man is simply terrified of being dragged into the abyss with you.”

Once again, Clark pulled out the paper. His eyes swept across the blue lettering. “Sodium Amytal.”
A pause ensued, in which only the dripping of a leaky faucet could be heard. “Every four hours.”

With a lightning-fast movement, Clark’s hand shot forward, grabbed the thick knot of Baker’s tie, and yanked the heavy man roughly toward him until their noses almost touched. “Did you spike Edgar’s coffee with that stuff, or was it the psychiatrist?”

“You… you’re assaulting a law enforcement officer,” Baker wheezed. His jugular vein bulged thickly. His face turned a dark red.

Clark opened his fist. Baker staggered backward, slammed against the wall, and gasped audibly for air. The shock, however, was short-lived. Baker straightened his uniform, patted the fabric smooth, and tried to regain his lost ground through sheer volume.

“You have absolutely nothing on me!” he thundered, raising a threatening index finger. “Those are pure fantasies. The pipe dreams of a desperate social worker trying to save that piece of trash Edgar from the gallows! It won’t do you any good. On the contrary, you’re going straight behind bars for assaulting an officer!”

Baker was working himself into a frenzy, his voice nearly cracking. Clark felt the familiar, old sense of guilt and shame rise within him, threatening to paralyze him like tar. He had to shut this down immediately.

“Listen to me carefully, Baker.” Clark drew himself up as tall as his spine would allow, squared his shoulders once more, and locked his eyes onto the man. “I don't give a damn what kind of mind games you used to soften his brain. The fact is, he was manipulated. By both of you. You are going back into that courtroom right now, and you are going to make sure Edgar is released. No arguments.”

“Like hell I will! I’m reporting you!” Baker thrust his index finger hard against Clark’s sternum.

Now or never,, Clark thought.

“Take your fingers off me right now,” he whispered, but his whisper was sharp and venomous, like an infected arrow.

It worked. Baker abruptly snapped his hand back. But the shock instantly gave way to a dangerous rage. Clark’s gaze flicked downward. Baker’s right hand was sliding slowly, almost imperceptibly, toward the leather holster at his hip. His thumb was already releasing the safety catch.

“I’d leave that right where it is,” Clark said, drawing out his words. “Unless you want this pretty photo printed on the front page of the evening paper tomorrow?”

He pulled a printout from his inside pocket and held it right before Baker’s eyes. It was the ranger’s shot. Crystal clear. A clearing in the woods; in it, a man in a duffle coat with a low-drawn hood, handing a bundle of papers and a set of keys to a stranger.

Baker froze mid-motion. His hand trembled slightly against the weapon. Clark breathed a silent sigh of relief. That had hit the mark. Baker was the hooded man.

“What… what is that supposed to be?” Baker scoffed. His voice sounded brittle.

“You know exactly what it is. Davies took your picture when you handed over the stolen data and the keys.” Every word echoed off the bare walls.

“The photo proves nothing! Absolutely nothing!” Baker spat out, trying to arrange his features into a wooden poker face. “Every young punk in town is wearing a light duffle coat like that these days. What are you trying to pin on me?”

Clark remained silent. He savored the stillness. Slowly, with theatrical deliberation, he raised his arm and pointed his finger precisely at the edge of the sergeant’s hand. “There are plenty of duffle coats, Baker. But only one person in this entire county has that distinctive, lentil-sized mole right on the edge of his right hand.”

Baker turned deathly pale. His lips grew thin and bluish. The trap had sprung.

Suddenly, the doorknob rattled violently. The door vibrated. Someone was pushing against it from the outside, impatient and loud. Reflexively, Clark snapped his head toward the door. A fatal mistake.

Baker seized that split second of distraction. Lunging forward, he tore the photo from Clark’s fingers with brute force and ripped the paper into tiny shreds with a few frantic movements. He bolted into the open stall, tossed the fragments into the bowl, and slammed the flush button. The water roared downward.

Clark recovered instantly. “Occupied!” he called out in a booming voice toward the locked entrance door. The rattling outside stopped, followed by the sound of shuffling footsteps fading away.

Then Clark stepped up to the stall door where Baker stood—breathing heavily, his gaze fixed triumphantly on the swirling water.

“That was entirely pointless,” Clark said quietly. “I have the negatives. And don’t worry: if anything happens to me — even a fraction of what befell poor Davies — you can bet your life that my people will hold you accountable. I’ve stored the negatives in a safe place. And believe me, those people know no mercy.”

Baker froze. His panting ceased for a moment. In his panic, he had forgotten the obvious — this miserable social worker knew his trade.

Clark turned away, walked over to the white basin, and turned on the chrome faucet. Cold water gushed out. He held his hands underneath, rubbing his palms together while looking into the stained mirror in front of him. Baker’s silhouette was reflected in the glass. The massive sergeant suddenly seemed strangely shrunken. Almost small.

“We could, of course, make a deal,” Clark murmured, as if talking to himself. He reached for the rough towel, dried his fingers, and slowly turned around. His eyes sought direct contact once more. “You let Edgar Tyler go. I keep the photo in the drawer. How does that sound to you, my old friend?”

He tossed the towel onto the counter and risked a glance at the face of his wristwatch. “Well. The fifteen minutes are almost up. Duty calls.”

Clark walked past the silent sergeant, unlocked the door, and stepped out into the court’s stuffy hallway. With weak knees, he strode back into the courtroom and sank heavily into his wooden chair. His hands were shaking; the adrenaline was slowly ebbed away. But he had no time to catch his breath. McAllroy already had him in his sights and was heading straight for his seat.

The saving grace came from the front. The bailiff's voice cut through the dull pounding in Clark's temples, harsh and unforgiving: “Please take your seats. Ladies and gentlemen, the court is back in session.”

The people in the room began to whisper, underscored by the scraping of chair legs on the parquet floor. Then the heavy wooden double doors fell into the latch, swallowing the last daylight from the corridor and tightening around Clark’s throat.

Edgar was led back to the defense table. His shoes squeaked on the linoleum—a pitiful sound that drove into Clark’s heart like needle pricks. The judge didn’t even look up. He merely leafed through the dusty case files before him. The paper rustled monotonously. “Edgar Tyler, since you were advised of your rights before the recess, we needn't repeat the procedure...” He paused briefly. His glasses slipped a fraction down the bridge of his nose. “So you stand by it and confess to having committed the act with murderous intent? Is that correct, Mr. Tyler?”

Nobody breathed. Clark least of all.

Edgar sat there. Completely rigid, his shoulders hunched as if waiting for a blow. His mother was knotting her handkerchief, her knuckles white with tension. A fat tear escaped the corner of Edgar’s eye, tracing a glistening path down his cheek. He swallowed. His Adam's apple moved with effort up and down. Then his chin dropped forward. A silent, wretched nod.

It was over.

Clark felt a crushing weight on his chest. An invisible noose that tightened with every inch Edgar’s chin sank. He clawed his fingers so hard into the edge of the oak table that the wood dug beneath his nails. Just so he wouldn’t shake. Just so he wouldn’t break down screaming.

It was all for nothing. . The realization slowly dawned on Clark. And it was even worse, he thought. Every late-night encouragement, every goddamn promise he had given this boy — he would be able to keep none of them. The blunt force of his own failure hit him like a punch to the stomach, making him instantly sick.

A glance caught him from the side. It was Eyleen. Her eyes were narrow slits full of cold contempt. She saw it. She saw the wet sweat pasting his shirt to his back, the fine, uncontrollable twitching of his eyelid.

Clark looked away, hysterically searching for something to hold onto, and glanced toward Liz. But she stared stubbornly straight ahead, her arms crossed before her chest like a protective wall. Her rejection was the final execution of his ego. The great savior. The dedicated social worker blinding everyone with his empathy, her posture read. And wasn't she right? In the end, he was nothing but a cowardly, unreliable weakling who talked big and now stood by, watching an innocent boy run to his ruin.

A burning wave of shame rushed into Clark’s face, scalding his cheeks and blinding him with self-loathing. He was a fraud. A phony through and through. He had given them hope, only to cast them even deeper into the mire.

Clark slid lower in his chair as his knees gave out. He bent far forward beneath the table, reached for the dangling end of his left shoelace, and pulled at it blindly—solely to hide his face from their stares. Here, beneath the edge of the table, he felt safer, yet it was also the grave of his pride. A single, stinging tear detached itself and soaked into the dark fabric of his trousers before he could even wipe it away. He just wanted to disappear. Become invisible. Die.

No one paid him any attention anymore. Everyone stared at Edgar, whose lips parted, dry and chapped. A soft wheeze escaped the boy, but fear strangled his throat. No words came out. Only a pitiful whimper that tore Clark’s heart to pieces.
