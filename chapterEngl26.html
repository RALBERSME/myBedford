Chapter 26:


Read the text aloud:



The coarse fabric of the hammock cut into Clark's shoulders, but he didn't move. Above him, the canopies of the old apple trees swayed. Lazy, warm summer light filtered through the leaves, dancing in restless patches across his skin. Somewhere, a thrush cried out. The wind carried the distant, oily scent of the highway, cooling the sweat on his forehead.

Hearing the clatter of porcelain, he blinked. The glare stung his eyes. Richard slid the heavy tray onto the wobbly wooden table in the seating area. The aroma of strong coffee and the tart note of freshly baked cheesecake dispelled the sweet haze of half-sleep. Clark had been resting here for three days now. He hadn't slept through the night like he did today since the image of the clearing and the grimace of the hooded man had shaken him awake, drenched in sweat.

He sat up slowly. His muscles felt as heavy as lead. His bare soles sank into the cool, dew-drenched grass as he slid out of the hammock. Every blade bent without resistance — a soft, almost painfully comforting contrast to the hard asphalt of the Bedford court corridors. He padded over to Richard and sank into the creaking wicker chair beside him.

"Richard." Clark stared at the cake plate his friend's calloused hand pushed toward him. His fingers trembled as they brushed the pastry fork. "You're just too good to me. What did I do to deserve this?"

Richard didn't answer right away. He merely smoothed the tablecloth. Only then did Clark realize what this man had become to him over the past few weeks. Richard's home had become a sort of fortress. The man himself a father figure who asked no questions when you knocked on his door bleeding in the dead of night, but simply fetched the first-aid kit in silence.

Clark slid a piece of cake into his mouth. It tasted of lemon and heavy batter, but the bite almost caught in his throat. "You know, Richard"—he stared at the brown crumbs on his plate—"these three days here... I've been thinking. I'm going back to Luton."

Richard paused. He stopped the coffee pot mid-pour; the dark stream cut off. "Back to Luton?" Wrinkles etched deep into his forehead, his eyes narrowing into slits. "But you've only just drawn a line under it all. For Bedford. Are you going to throw everything away just like that? Clark?"

Clark chewed mechanically. "In Bedford, everyone thinks I'm a failure." The word tasted more bitter than the coffee. "My landlady crosses the street when she sees me. The onlookers in the courtroom whispered about me. McAllroy spits when I walk by. Eyleen won't even look at me, and..." He trailed off. The fork scraped sharply against the plate.

"And who else, Clark?" Richard's voice was a low rumble—calm but unyielding.

"And Liz doesn't want anything to do with me either." A sharp pain crept up his temples.

"Is that so?" Richard pressed.

"I think so." Clark fiddled with the hem of his shirt, tugging at a loose thread until the fabric tautened. "She only looked at me sideways in court. So cold. As if I were dirt." He took a deep breath, his chest heaving unevenly. "But there's something else. I asked her for a favor. When I had to hand over the photos and negatives in the clearing. I thought I needed an insurance policy, a witness in the bushes in case things went sideways. In case I didn't make it back." He swallowed with difficulty, his throat dry.

The porcelain clattered. Richard had set his plate down hard. "You did what?"

"I sent her an urgent message. Told her to hide in the fir thicket. Behind the large elderberry."

"And did she come?" Richard wanted to know, leaning his upper body far forward.

"I don't know." Clark shrugged his shoulders, shrinking back into his chair. "It was too dark. I was panicking completely. After the handover, I just ran, jumped on my bike, the tires spinning in the mud. I thought the hooded man would send his people after me to finish me off. I didn't even look back."

"And Liz?" Richard sounded startled. His hand rested on Clark's shoulder, a gesture that said, you can trust me with anything.

"I don't know." Clark clenched his jaw.

"If she did come... she risked a hell of a lot, don't you think?" Richard interjected.

Clark nodded and continued, "I blame myself terribly. The guy... he shot. Right in the direction where I thought Liz was. The snapping of the branches is still ringing in my ears. The next morning, I went straight to Midland Road. I hid around the corner of the building until I saw her come out of her sewing shop. Fortunately, she was unhurt. Only then did I bike over to you."

"Good. She's alive." Richard poured more coffee; pale steam rose.

"I'm going back today," Clark stammered, the words tumbling over each other. "I’ll talk to her. Tomorrow I’ll visit Edgar—he’s bound to need some emotional support after that nightmare of a trial. And then..." He trailed off.

Richard’s expression had hardened. No nodding. No approval.

"Clark." A gentle tone, yet it stood like a brick wall. "Don't you see it?"

"See what?" Clark’s voice cracked. He bolted upright, his chair scraping loudly across the stone pavers. "I am responsible for these damn people! I let them down. I should be in Bedford right now, cleaning up the mess, sweeping up the shattered pieces I caused!"

"No, Clark." Richard remained seated, perfectly calm, looking up at him. "It's time you finally found yourself."

An abrupt silence fell over them.

"Your entire life, you've been worrying about everyone else. The boys in Luton. The Tylers in Bedford. Always running. But where do you fit into all this? You’re not running from the hooded man, son. You’re running from yourself," Richard made clear.

The sentence hit Clark like a punch to the gut. He forgot to breathe. Responsibility, the word flared up inside him. That was the word his father had beaten into him with a leather belt. You are responsible. Always.

Clark had learned to read the shifting energy of a room before anyone even spoke. He would notice the tremor of a hand, the flicker of an eye, and step in to help. Weekends? Holidays? His door in Luton had never been locked. The boys had come with their broken souls whenever they needed to, and he had pieced them back together. And now that was supposed to be wrong? The thought felt like high treason against his very self.

He sank back into his chair and set the plate down. The minutes stretched like rubber. The chirping of the birds suddenly sounded shrill, almost mocking.

Richard finally broke the silence without looking at Clark. His eyes were fixed on the distant horizon beyond the apple trees. "Just look inside yourself for a moment. When you think of Bedford... what were you hoping for there?"

Clark stared at his toes in the grass. They were dirty. He reflected. "I wanted out," he whispered. "Out of my old skin. To do something of my own. To write stories. Funny things that make people laugh." He sniffled, wiping his nose with the back of his hand. "I wanted to fall in love, Richard. To start a family of my own. Not just constantly absorb everyone else's problems."

Richard's warm hand settled over Clark's trembling fingers. "And how far did you get?"

"I wrote." Clark took a deep breath, tears now streaming freely down his face. "The pieces were good. Truly. I thought they were witty. But nobody wanted them. The newspaper sent them back unread. And Eyleen... well, I couldn't reach Eyleen either. She pushed me away."

"And what else?" Richard urged him on.

"Suppose" — Richard pressed further, his voice dropping lower, almost conspiratorial — "suppose this Liz had been in the clearing that night. Just as a thought experiment. She takes on this deadly risk, sits in the wet leaves, and sticks her neck out for you. What do you think that means?"

Richard stood up. He picked up the tray and walked back into the house without another word. The screen door clicked shut behind him.

Clark was left alone.

In his mind’s eye, the scene played out once more. Again, he saw the clearing, smelled the rotting wood and the gunpowder smoke. But this time, the picture shifted. He saw her: Liz. She was lying on her stomach in the damp moss. She was enveloped in the heavy military uniform that was far too large for her narrow shoulders. The camera lens lay heavy in her hands, her knuckles white with tension. She was trembling with fear, but she pressed the shutter and snapped the evidence photos. She held her breath, scared out of her wits when the hooded man raised the gun. Then the shot that shattered the pine bark right above her head. Wood splinters hit her face, which was filled with sheer panic. And after that: that endless, pitch-black night. Ten kilometers of no man's land. She had dragged herself onto that old bicycle, making the journey back to Bedford all by herself. For him.

She had risked her life. And he? The next morning, he had merely made sure she was still breathing and then bolted. No thank you, not a single word had he exchanged with her. Yet Liz had always been there. She had brewed tea, she had listened to him, even when he remained silent.

A sudden jolt of electricity shot through Clark's limbs. His feelings for her were not mere gratitude. It was much more. It was exactly what he had been searching for all along.

Clark sprang up. His knees were steady now. Richard was right. Screw Luton. Screw the old roles. This was about his future. Right now.

He ran to the fence where his bicycle leaned against the tangled hedge. A moment later, he was already stomping on the pedals until the chain snapped taut. He didn't look back. The rushing wind blew the last doubts from his mind as he tore down the hill. Back to Bedford. Back to Midland Road. Back to the little sewing shop where, hopefully, the light was still burning.
