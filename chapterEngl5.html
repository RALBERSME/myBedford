Chapter 5:


Read the text aloud:



The next day, Clark treated himself to a break. He decided to use the time to let the fresh wind clear his head in the open countryside around Bedford. It would surely spark new thoughts — thoughts he so desperately needed.

The wind carried the crisp scent of damp earth and freshly felled pine across the hills of Bedfordshire. Clark pulled the collar of his tweed jacket higher as he followed the narrow path separating the dense woods north of the town from the open meadows. It was the 3rd of June, 1953. Only yesterday, the entire country had revelled in the euphoria of the new Queen; colorful bunting still hung everywhere from farm fences. But today, on this chilly Wednesday morning, everyday life had returned with a vengeance. And with it, Clark's crushing loneliness.

At thirty-six, he often felt like a man caught between eras. The war years had left deep scars on his soul, and the happiness he so desperately sought always seemed just out of reach. Eyleen had not reciprocated his feelings; the sting of the previous evening still burned deep in his chest. Every step on the soft forest floor felt like a betrayal of his dreams. He had fled — escaping the claustrophobia of the town into the great outdoors, just to breathe freely again.

At the edge of the large forestry estate, he paused. The monotonous, screeching whine of a circular saw pierced the morning stillness. Looking through the trees, he caught sight of the local sawmill. Bedford Sawmill was painted there in bold lettering. The place was a hive of activity: men with soot-stained faces and heavy leather belts hoisted massive logs onto wooden sledges. The rhythmic thud of axes, the splintering of bark, and the loud shouting of the woodsmen filled the air. They swore, laughed, and sweated for their daily bread. A young laborer, barely twenty, wiped the sweat from his brow. He caught Clark’s eye and offered a nod of greeting before shoveling away the next load of sawdust. This effortless, raw vitality of men starting a new workday without hesitation dealt a quiet sting to Clark’s melancholy heart. It was not painful, but it reminded him of his time in Luton, when he too had always been ready whenever his boys needed him. And they needed him almost always.

Leaving the woods, he stepped out onto the wide meadows rolling in gentle waves toward the horizon. The morning mist still lay like a thin lace blanket over the lush green. Bedfordshire showed its most innocent side. Wildflowers, blue speedwell, and white campion swayed in the gentle breeze. On the distant edge of the fields rose the giant chimneys of the brickworks and industrial plants of Stewartby. Even from here, the dull rumble of machinery could be heard.

Clark knew who worked over there. In recent years, hundreds of young Italian migrant workers had come to the region to stoke the kilns and rebuild the country. Was the father of the little Italian boy who had run in front of the bus on the day of his arrival toiling there too? Clark pushed the thought aside. Today, he was determined to forget all his troubles.

Taking a break by the wayside, he watched a group of workers changing shifts, crossing the path toward the railway tracks. They spoke rapid, melodic Italian, gesturing wildly and laughing aloud. One of them held a worn photograph of a woman, kissing it with immense pride and showing it to his comrades. A shiver ran down Clark’s spine. Instantly, his mind drifted back to Eyleen. But he refused to dwell on last night's scene. He looked back at the other men. Despite the grueling, dusty labor in a foreign land, an unquenchable fire burned within them. They brought a warmth and passion to an often chilly, reserved England that was almost impossible to resist.

Clark breathed in deeply. The scent of wild thyme mingled with the distant coal smoke from the factories. He looked at his own hands. Why was he letting grief paralyze him? The world around him was not standing still. Nature was blooming in lavish splendor, the woodsmen were building the future, and the Italians carried the love for their families like a protective shield.

A shaft of sudden sunlight broke through the cloud cover, bathing the meadows in a warm, golden glow. In that moment, Clark felt the chill in his chest begin to thaw. His heart was still beating. It beat in time with this beautiful, vibrant landscape. His life was not over — it was only just beginning to find its rhythm again. He turned around and walked on with firmer, quicker strides. He was ready to look the world in the eye once more. And somewhere out there, the love meant for him was waiting.


It was nearly dark when he returned to Ashburnham Road. At the corner kiosk, he tossed a few coins to the vendor and pulled the latest edition of the Bedfordshire Standard from the stack. He wanted to see if his report on yesterday's coronation had been printed.

In a flash, he tore out the arts section — the Poet’s Corner. The light from the gas lamp was insufficient, simply too dim. The letters blurred before his watery eyes. He groaned. It was no use; he had to go up to his flat.

On the top step outside his front door lay something white. A letter, slightly creased. Clark bent down, slotted the key into the lock, and tossed the paper onto the table next to the Remington typewriter, along with the newspaper. Then he peeled off his sweaty clothes and slipped into his pyjamas.

Only then did he sit down at the table. His fingers trembled as he leafed through the torn-out arts section. His eyes scanned the columns like a hawk on the hunt. First page — nothing. Second — nothing either. The tension mounted as the stack of paper in his left hand grew thinner. When he turned the final page only to find advertisements, he slumped in his chair.

Anxiety gnawed at him. Had he missed something? he asked himself in a panic. He leafed back. Faster. Once more, his eyes raced through every page. Yet it changed nothing: not a single word of his had been published. Clark snorted through his nose. "Tomorrow," he whispered into the empty room to calm himself. "They're surely printing it tomorrow."

Pushing the supplement aside, he reached for the letter and smoothed the crease in the envelope. Using a pair of scissors, he cleanly sliced open the edge. As he pulled out the sheet, he spotted the red stamp of the Bedfordshire Standard. His heart skipped a beat. So, they had read it.

His eyes flew across the lines. "...we regret to inform you that articles of this nature do not..."
Humiliated, Clark folded the paper. "Rejected. No interest in my first work as an author. All that effort for nothing," he lamented.

He exhaled deeply. What a wretched day. And yet it had begun so regally. Now all he wanted was to sleep, to forget everything. He grabbed the letter along with the arts pages and stuffed them carelessly onto the stack of newspapers. Then he turned the pile over so that the front page of today's edition faced neatly upward. He was just about to stand up when the bold, black headline caught his eye:

"Son of Former Musical Star Eyleen Newland a Murderer?"

A cold dread ran down Clark's spine. He snatched the article. The paper crackled violently in his grip as he read the first few lines. This couldn't be true. "My Eyleen!" he groaned.

His fingers dug deeper and deeper into the edges, leaving gray smudges of newsprint on the cheap groundwood paper. He read the cruel accusation for a fifth time, then a sixth. His eyes traced the narrow rows of lead type—stubbornly, mechanically—while his mind swallowed the words like jagged stones.

Edgar Tyler.

The name blared in bold black against the yellowed white. He was only twenty-two years old. Eyleen Newland’s boy—the son of a woman who had once stood in the spotlight, her smile beaming from theater posters before the glamour was choked out by the dust of Bedford. Now her son was making headlines. A murderer, they called him. Or so it read, even if the local reporter had shrouded the allegation in a dense layer of subjunctives to shield himself legally. Edgar was said to have killed George Davies at his own sawmill. Davies, the article noted, was the local forester. He was described as a patriarch with resin-stained hands and a gruff, commanding tone, who owned the small mill at the edge of the woods. Edgar had been his sole employee. That made him the prime suspect.

Yesterday, on June 3rd, Davies had been found covered in blood — nothing more than a lump of flesh executed amidst fresh sawdust, directly inside the mechanism of his own saw. The editorial staff had omitted a photograph. A matter of sheer decency, the text claimed. Instead, they featured Edgar’s face. A mugshot, coarsely halftoned and heavily enlarged. He stared mutely out of the newspaper. His gaze looked caught, helpless, trapped between the columns. Edgar was gone. On the run since the deed.

Naturally, the text maintained that guilt was yet unproven. The journalist poked around regardless. A seasoned man like Davies did not simply fall into the spinning teeth of his own machine. And then, of course, there was the jacket. It was Edgar’s work jacket. Heavy blue denim, stiff with sweat and grime. It lay on a chopping block just a few paces from the pool of blood, as though he had discarded it in a hurry to run faster. The article ranted about an unprecedented crime. Bedford was a backwater. Here, people died of old age or in a drunken stupor, but nobody got dismembered. At exactly 4:00 PM, police had cordoned off the crime scene. A large force had been digging through the sawdust ever since, hunting for forensics. Anyone with useful information was urged to report directly to Sergeant Aaron Baker, who was leading the investigation. The reporter emphasized that there was particular reason to believe the suspect was a flight risk.

Clark was suffocating. Though he was seated, he felt the floor give way beneath him. He staggered to the sofa and let himself collapse, devoid of strength. The springs groaned dryly under his weight. His skin felt taut, cold, and waxen. The blood seemed to drain entirely from his limbs. The scent of damp wood and death crept virtually from the printed type straight into his nostrils. His stomach churned. A spasm rose inside him. He clenched his teeth, but the bitter taste of bile was quicker. There was no time to reach the bathroom; the distance was too great. In a rush, Clark grabbed the wastepaper basket beside the table.

With a violent heave, the tension emptied out. Clark trembled. A thin thread of saliva hung from his chin as he raised his head, gasping for air. A wave of relief followed the physical revulsion, but it was minimal, barely perceptible. With the back of his hand, he wiped his mouth, smearing the cold sweat across his upper lip, and pulled himself together. His knees felt like soft wax. He held the basket far out in front of him as he stumbled around the corner. The bathroom smelled of lye soap and damp towels. He dumped the contents of the basket into the toilet, flushed twice, and scrubbed the tin under the gurgling tap. The water was ice-cold. He splashed it onto his face, drinking a few gulps directly from his cupped hand. Then he sank onto the hard, enameled rim of the bathtub.

"Why on earth did I come to Bedford?" he asked himself, wallowing in self-pity.

Everything had been easier in Luton. His boys were there. He had left them behind, properly settled, cared for, their files closed. And now, another twist of fate was waiting here to sweep the legs out from under him. His thoughts broke off, drifting backward into the years of smoky offices and wet cobblestones. He saw the youths before him again—boys in oversized jackets and with too much anger in their bellies. He had picked them up off the asphalt. Some with sticky fingers from shoplifting, others with cheap hashish in their socks, caught by beat cops in dark alleyways. He had talked, for hours on end, as if his life depended on it.

He had always possessed that eye for seeing the tremor behind the boys’ cool bravado. He knew about the broken homes they had run away from, and the hunger for anything that resembled respect. He didn't mend laws. He patched up people. And some returned—years later, as grown men. They would knock on his door, hat in hand, their eyes flickering with embarrassment. "Thank you, Mr. Clark." That was the phrase that had always given him renewed strength. A thin balm for a heart he had long since mothballed for his own sake. There had been no room for a life of his own among all that foreign wreckage.

"But this?" It was too much for him. This wasn't a stolen bicycle. This wasn't a packet of tobacco from the corner shop. This was iron and blood, a cold-blooded murder—and that word had never before existed in his files.

Twenty-two years. Edgar was no longer a child, but he wasn't quite a man either. Clark stared at the white tiles of the bathroom. Not until twenty-five, or perhaps twenty-two at the earliest—so the medical journals from the universities stated—did the pathways in the brain fully connect. Only then did the foundation of the psyche set as firm as concrete. At twenty-two, everything was still volatile. In the eyes of the British Crown, Edgar had been fully competent to conduct business for a mere twelve months. A single year of adulthood. One solitary year to learn how not to become a monster.

What exactly had happened at that sawmill?

If Edgar had struck the blow, fleeing was the logical consequence. Pure panic, like an animal bolting into the undergrowth because the light grows too bright. Guilt, shame — it ate you alive. But had a crime even taken place? Clark pushed himself up from the edge of the tub and walked back into the living room. The newspaper still lay there, crumpled. He read the lines again, searching for the gaps between the words.

Nothing was proven. Not a single fact.

Everything rested on the writer's use of the subjunctive. Conjectures. The inflated theories of a man in need of lineage rates. And the opinion of Baker. Clark struck the article with his fist. The wood of the table shuddered; the paper tore beneath his knuckles.

"Baker!" That damned sergeant with the thick neck and the small eyes was the pest of this town. A shadow that fell over anyone who had ever stumbled. He would hunt Edgar down like a piece of game — not for justice, but for the promotion. Of that, Clark was absolutely certain.

The grandfather clock in the hallway struck two. Two solitary, deep chimes that faded into the empty room.

Clark paced back and forth. The rug muffled his steps as he walked between the window and the door. The social worker he still was on the inside refused to sit silently in his armchair. His blood demanded action. And then there was Eyleen. The face of an inconsolable mother imposed itself over Edgar's wanted poster. An old promise burning in Clark's chest. It stung — doubly so.

The hours dragged on like thick tar. Staying awake became a physical battle; his eyelids burned as if made of sandpaper. As the chill of the early morning hours began to creep through the cracks in the window frame, his body finally gave out. He didn’t collapse; he simply sank sideways onto the cushion of the worn-out sofa, his legs tucked in, an arm pillowed beneath his head. Sleep came heavy and devoid of dreams.
