Chapter 33:


Read the text aloud:



The silence between the two women stretched on endlessly. Only when a key turned in the lock and the front door swung open did life return to the house. Martha, the old cleaning lady, stepped into the parlor. She had pulled her worn headscarf deep down over her forehead and carried a bucket full of scrubbing rags. She caught herself in the middle of the hallway, visibly surprised to find the two women in the dimly lit living room.

"Oh, Mrs. Tyler, Liz," Martha croaked, setting the bucket down. "I thought the house was empty. Such a shame about the Sergeant. A real shame."

"A bitter loss for us all, Martha," Eyleen replied instantly. Her voice slid back into its theatrical tone of mourning in the blink of an eye. "The world has grown a little darker."

Martha gave a soft snort, sniffled, and rubbed her hands on her apron. "Well. Darker, maybe. But if I'm being honest, the Lord Sergeant was hardly recognizable lately. If you look at it right, the nice, friendly man from before had turned into a real tyrant. Always ruling over everyone, always criticizing. You couldn't do anything right by him. Every single thing I did was wrong." She shook her head, stepped a bit closer, and eyed Eyleen’s perfect outfit. "But you know how he could be. You used to come around here more often in the past. Much more often."

Eyleen tensed. Her eyelids twitched.

Martha didn't notice. She blew her nose into a handkerchief and stared thoughtfully into space. "But to marry — that Clark fellow wanted to marry you, didn't he? Why did that never work out between the two of you anyway? He's a nice guy, that Clark. Isn't he?"

The air in the parlor seemed to grow thin. Liz stared from the cleaning lady to her mother. Eyleen was visibly infuriated by this gossipmonger.

"I’d better go check on the windows," Martha muttered, having apparently picked up on the ice age in the room. She grabbed her bucket, shuffled up the stairs, and disappeared onto the upper floor.

"What did she mean?" Liz gasped. Her voice trembled.

Eyleen did not answer. Her gaze was locked onto Liz’s hand. More precisely, onto the white, unopened letter Dr. Vance had left her. Eyleen’s breathing quickened. Her nostrils flared with a mixture of sheer terror and simmering rage. She feared the worst. She could smell the secret that now threatened to rise from that envelope like a foul vapor.

"Give me the letter," Eyleen demanded, her hand imperiously outstretched.

"No," Liz countered, stepping back and pressing the paper to her chest. "It belongs to me. Dr. Vance gave it to me. What is going on here, Mother? Why did Aaron Baker leave me this house?"

Eyleen let out a bitter, ugly laugh. The mask of the mourning primadonna finally shattered. What remained was a woman eaten away by malice. "So you want to know? You little, naive goose, do you really believe in a miracle? Do you honestly think he made you rich because of your oh-so-wonderful curtains?" She stepped menacingly close to Liz. "You are Aaron’s daughter, Liz. That is the whole secret."

The words hit Liz like a physical blow. Her knees buckled. She collapsed into the nearest upholstered chair, the air escaping her lungs with a faint gasp. "What...?"

"An affair," Eyleen said coldly, almost casually, as she picked an invisible lint fiber off her black silk. "Aaron and I. Before you were born, it was quite a passionate thing."

Liz shook her head—a violent, unbelieving shake. "No. No, that’s not true. Father... Edward..."

"Edward was an idiot!" Eyleen cut her off. Her voice was entirely devoid of remorse. She stepped up to the chair and placed a hand on Liz’s shoulder—a gesture meant to look like comfort, but possessing the coldness of a tombstone. "I passed you off to Edward as a cuckoo's egg. He never suspected a thing. He raised you believing you were his own flesh and blood. And you know what? No one else knows about this except the two of us in this room. And no one ever will."

Liz felt the blood rushing in her ears. A cuckoo’s child. A bastard. In this stifling little nest called Bedford, that wasn't just a blemish—it was a social death sentence. A disgrace to be dragged through the mud. Her entire identity, the memory of the kind father who had fallen in London during the war, everything shattered into a thousand sharp shards.

"Don't wet your pants, Liz," Eyleen said, giving her cheek a rough pat. "Edward is dead. Aaron is dead. Who is going to notice? Don't make such a fuss. You have a house now. Be grateful."

Eyleen turned on her heel, smoothed her dress, and walked toward the door. "I’m going home. It’s getting too suffocating in here. Don't dawdle." She strutted out of the house without even pulling the door shut behind her. Liz was left alone in the gloomy living room.

The silence was crushing. Liz stared at the picture on the wall. The 45th birthday. Aaron, laughing. Her biological father. A man who, according to the cleaning lady, had ended up a domineering tyrant — just like Eyleen.

Everything spun inside Liz. Her worldview, neatly ordered into good and evil, into hard tailoring work and fair wages, had imploded within minutes. She held the letter in her hands, but she didn't have the strength to open it. She didn't even have the strength to stand up.
